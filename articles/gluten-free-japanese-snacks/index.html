Gluten-Free Japanese Snacks: Rice Crackers, Mochi, Wagashi

November 3, 2026 · 7 min read

Japan’s snack aisles are full of things that look naturally gluten-free — rice crackers, mochi, little bean-paste sweets — and some genuinely are. But “made from rice” doesn’t mean “safe to eat if you have coeliac disease,” because Japanese snack-making uses wheat in ways that aren’t obvious from the name of the sweet. The short answer: plain rice- and bean-based snacks are often wheat-free, but you need to check the ingredient type, the glaze, and the dusting powder every single time, because it varies by brand and even by flavour within the same brand.

This guide breaks down the three big snack categories — senbei-style rice crackers, mochi, and wagashi — so you know what to look for before you put something in your mouth.

Why “it’s just rice” isn’t enough

Three things cause most of the surprises:

Soy sauce glaze. Regular Japanese soy sauce (koikuchi shōyu) is brewed from wheat and soybeans, and it’s the single most common source of hidden wheat in rice crackers. A cracker can be 100% rice flour and still contain wheat because it was brushed with soy sauce before baking. We cover this in detail in Is Soy Sauce Gluten-Free in Japan? — the short version is that wheat-free soy sauce exists but is a minority product, so don’t assume.

Starch substitution. Some traditional sweets are supposed to use starches like bracken starch (warabiko) or potato starch (katakuriko), but cheaper modern versions quietly substitute wheat starch because it’s cheaper and gives a similar texture. The name of the sweet doesn’t change, so you can’t tell from the label on the shop sign.

Shared equipment. Rice cracker and wagashi makers often produce wheat-containing and wheat-free items in the same workshop, using the same dusting flour, the same frying oil, or the same conveyor line. For coeliac disease, that cross-contact matters even when the final ingredient list looks clean.

If you have coeliac disease or another serious reaction to wheat, carry any prescribed medication as advised by your doctor, and treat that advice as separate from — not replaced by — the tips in this article.

Rice crackers: senbei, okaki and arare

These are the crunchy, often round or stick-shaped crackers sold everywhere from supermarkets to souvenir shops. The base is almost always rice (either mochi-gome glutinous rice or regular rice), but what’s on the rice is where wheat shows up.

Type Usually wheat-free? Watch for
Shio (salt) senbei Often yes Check for soy sauce in the ingredient list anyway
Shōyu (soy sauce) senbei Usually contains wheat Regular soy sauce = wheat, unless labelled wheat-free
Zarame (sugar-coated) senbei Varies Some glazes include soy sauce or wheat-based seasoning
Arare / okaki (bite-sized) Varies widely Mixed bags often include both plain and soy-flavoured pieces
Kaki no tane style mixes Usually contains wheat Often blended with wheat crackers or peanuts in the same bag

A few brands do make dedicated wheat-free soy sauce senbei using tamari or a labelled wheat-free shōyu, and these are worth seeking out specifically — they’re not the default, so look for explicit labelling rather than assuming any “soy sauce flavour” cracker qualifies.

How to check: For packaged crackers bought in a shop or convenience store, Japan’s mandatory allergen labelling system requires wheat (小麦, komugi) to be listed if it’s a genuine ingredient, so scan the ingredient panel for that word. For background on how this labelling system works and its limits, see Japanese Food Allergy Labels: The 9 Mandatory Allergens Explained. If you’re buying from a convenience store specifically, our guide to reading konbini labels walks through exactly where on the packet to look.

For crackers sold loose — from a jar at a souvenir shop, a department store food hall counter, or a market stall — there is no mandatory allergen labelling at all. In that situation, ask directly. A useful phrase here is 小麦は使っていますか？ (Komugi wa tsukatte imasu ka?) — “Does this contain wheat?”

Mochi: the clearest case, with exceptions

Plain mochi — pounded glutinous rice, nothing else — is one of the more reliably wheat-free traditional foods in Japan, because its whole identity is built on rice’s stickiness, not wheat’s. That’s the good news.

The exceptions cluster around three things:

  • Dusting powder. Fresh mochi is often dusted with starch so it doesn’t stick to hands or trays. This is usually potato starch (katakuriko) or cornstarch, but occasionally it’s wheat starch, especially in mass-produced or lower-cost products. The dusting powder isn’t always listed prominently because it’s a small quantity, so check the ingredients rather than assuming.
  • Fillings and coatings. Daifuku (mochi stuffed with sweet bean paste) is typically fine on the mochi-and-filling front, but some varieties are coated in or served alongside wheat-based biscuit crumbs, cookie pieces, or cake layers as a modern twist — these are usually marketed as a fusion product, so they tend to stand out, but check anyway.
  • Warabimochi. This jelly-like sweet is traditionally made from warabiko, a starch extracted from bracken root, which is wheat-free. However, genuine warabiko is expensive and slow to make, so many commercial warabimochi products substitute wheat starch, potato starch, or a blend — and the label doesn’t always announce which. This is one of the most common silent swaps in the wagashi world, so don’t assume “warabimochi” automatically means bracken starch.

Kinako, the toasted soybean flour often dusted over mochi, is itself wheat-free — it’s made from soybeans, not wheat — but it’s frequently processed on equipment shared with wheat-containing products, so cross-contact is a reasonable question to raise if your reaction to wheat is severe.

Wagashi: a genuinely mixed category

Wagashi is the broad umbrella term for traditional Japanese sweets, and it spans both ends of the gluten spectrum — which is exactly why it needs the most individual checking.

Often wheat-free:

  • Yōkan — a firm jellied sweet made from red bean paste (anko) and agar, with no flour involved in the traditional recipe
  • Dango — small rice-flour dumplings, often skewered and glazed; check the glaze for soy sauce
  • Daifuku — mochi with bean paste filling, with the caveats above about dusting starch

Often contain wheat:

  • Manju — steamed or baked buns where the outer skin is typically made with wheat flour, even though the filling is bean paste
  • Dorayaki — two small pancakes (wheat flour batter) sandwiching bean paste
  • Kasutera (castella) — a sponge cake with Portuguese origins, made with wheat flour, eggs and sugar
  • Monaka — wafer-shell sweets where the shell is usually a thin wheat-based wafer

Because the two groups look similarly “traditional Japanese,” you genuinely cannot guess from appearance or name alone which category a given shop’s version falls into — some shops make a rice-flour version of something that’s normally wheat-based. This is where asking pays off, especially at wagashi specialty shops where staff will usually know their own recipes well, unlike a convenience store clerk who may only know what’s printed on the packet.

How to ask, in person

For any situation where you’re buying from a counter, a market stall, or a traditional sweet shop rather than reading a printed label, start the conversation clearly:

  • すみません、食事制限があります。 (Sumimasen, shokuji seigen ga arimasu.) — “Excuse me, I have dietary restrictions.”
  • 小麦が食べられません。 (Komugi ga taberaremasen.) — “I can’t eat wheat.”
  • If coeliac disease is the reason, it’s worth being specific: セリアック病のため、少量の小麦でも体調が悪くなります。 (Seriakku-byō no tame, shōryō no komugi demo taichō ga waruku narimasu.) — “I have coeliac disease, so even a small amount of wheat makes me ill.”
  • For a specific snack: 小麦は使っていますか？ (Komugi wa tsukatte imasu ka?) — “Does this contain wheat?”
  • If soy sauce glaze is a possibility: 小麦不使用の醤油はありますか？ (Komugi fushiyō no shōyu wa arimasu ka?) — “Do you have wheat-free soy sauce?”
  • To close politely once you’ve got your answer: 大丈夫です、ありがとうございます。 (Daijōbu desu, arigatō gozaimasu.) — “That’s fine, thank you.”

Buying with more confidence

Packaged snacks sold in supermarkets and convenience stores are your most reliable starting point, because Japan’s mandatory allergen system requires wheat to be declared when it’s a genuine ingredient — look for 小麦 printed in the ingredient list or in a separate allergen summary near it. That system has some real gaps worth knowing: it only applies to pre-packaged food, not to anything sold loose over a counter, and it doesn’t address shared-equipment cross-contact at all. It’s a useful filter, not a guarantee.

If you’re deciding between a konbini run and a specialty wagashi shop, the konbini generally gives you more printed information to work with upfront; the wagashi shop gives you a human who can answer specific questions but no printed allergen panel. Neither is inherently safer — they just require different approaches.

Quick checklist

  • Plain salt rice crackers (shio senbei) and plain mochi are more often wheat-free, but confirm every brand — don’t generalise from one packet to all.
  • Soy sauce flavouring (shōyu senbei, glazed dango) usually means wheat unless the product explicitly states wheat-free soy sauce.
  • Warabimochi and other starch-based sweets sometimes substitute wheat starch for the traditional starch — check, don’t assume from the name.
  • Wagashi splits sharply: yōkan and dango are typically flour-free, while manju, dorayaki, kasutera and monaka are typically wheat-based.
  • Packaged snacks must list wheat if present; loose-sold snacks at counters and stalls have no such requirement, so ask directly.
  • Carry the key phrases above, and if your reaction to wheat is severe, ask about shared equipment, not just the ingredient list.
  • Always carry any medication your doctor has prescribed for accidental exposure, and follow their guidance — this article is general information, not medical advice.
