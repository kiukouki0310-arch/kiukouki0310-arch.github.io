Gluten-Free at Japanese Convenience Stores: Reading Konbini Labels

October 22, 2026 · 6 min read

Convenience stores (konbini) are often the first place jet-lagged, hungry travellers turn to in Japan, and the good news is that packaged konbini food actually comes with proper allergen labelling — unlike most restaurant food. The catch is knowing what that labelling does and doesn’t tell you, and knowing where wheat likes to hide in products that look like plain rice or vegetables.

This guide walks through how Japanese food labelling law applies to konbini shelves, which product categories are riskiest for gluten, and exactly what to look for before you unwrap anything.

Why konbini labelling is actually useful

Restaurant meals and loose food sold over a counter have no mandatory allergen labelling in Japan — staff knowledge is all you get. But pre-packaged food sold in stores, including konbini onigiri, sandwiches, salads and snacks, falls under Japan’s food labelling law. That means the nine allergens the government requires manufacturers to declare must appear somewhere on the pack if they’re used as an ingredient:

egg, milk, wheat, buckwheat, peanut, shrimp, crab, walnut (mandatory since April 2025), and cashew nut (mandatory from April 2026, with a transition period running to March 2028 — so some stock on shelves now may still predate the cashew requirement and simply not mention it).

Two allergens worth knowing about sit outside the mandatory list: barley is not required to be declared at all, and macadamia and pistachio are only “recommended,” not mandatory. If any of these matter to you, you can’t rely on the absence of a mention — you need to check the full ingredient list, not just the allergen summary.

Importantly, this mandatory labelling only covers declared allergens as deliberate ingredients. It does not cover cross-contact. A rice ball made on a production line that also handles wheat-containing products is not required to carry a “may contain traces of wheat” warning the way some other countries’ packaging does. So a clean ingredient list tells you what went in on purpose — it doesn’t rule out shared equipment.

If your coeliac disease means even trace amounts cause a reaction, that distinction matters a lot, and it’s worth reading our guide on Japanese food allergy labels and the nine mandatory allergens for the full legal picture before you rely on any packet.

Where wheat actually hides in konbini food

The instinctive assumption — “it’s rice, so it’s fine” — is exactly where gluten-free shoppers get caught out in Japan. Regular soy sauce (koikuchi shōyu) is brewed with wheat, and it turns up as a seasoning in an enormous number of products that don’t look like soy sauce dishes at all.

Category Looks safe, but… What to check
Onigiri (rice balls) Pickled plum (umeboshi) and plain salmon fillings are often low-risk, but tuna mayo, kombu, and seasoned fillings frequently use soy-sauce-based seasoning Full ingredient list, not just the filling name
Salads Vegetables themselves are gluten-free, but the dressing packet is the real risk — soy-sauce, sesame and “wafū” (Japanese-style) dressings almost always contain wheat Dressing ingredients separately, even if sold in its own small sachet
Bento boxes Multiple components (rice, protein, pickles, sauce) each have their own risk; teriyaki, karaage and simmered dishes usually rely on soy sauce or a wheat-thickened sauce Each component’s ingredient list, and whether sauces are mixed in or separate
Rice crackers / senbei Despite being rice-based, many senbei are glazed or seasoned with soy sauce Ingredient list; some brands do make wheat-free versions, but it varies
Sandwiches Bread is the obvious wheat, but even “salad” fillings can include a soy-sauce-seasoned component Not usually a viable option at all for gluten-free, bread aside
Hot case items (fried chicken, oden, nikuman) Shared fryers and shared broth are common; frying batter is wheat-based These sit outside packaged labelling rules in some stores — ask staff or avoid

Miso, used in some dressings, soups and simmered dishes, and dashi (stock) bases can also carry wheat depending on the brand and style, so treat anything described as “miso-flavoured” or containing a mixed seasoning sauce with the same caution as soy sauce.

How to actually read the label

Japanese packaging generally lists ingredients in one block (原材料名, genzairyō-mei), roughly in descending order by weight, often with the allergen summary either woven into that list in brackets or given as a short separate line near it. Look specifically for the kanji 小麦 (komugi, wheat) and 醤油 (shōyu, soy sauce) — these are the two words worth training your eye to spot quickly, since soy sauce is both an allergen-containing ingredient in its own right and a very common carrier of hidden wheat.

A few practical habits:

  • Photograph the ingredient list before you decide, so you can translate it properly later if the shelf lighting or your Japanese is making it hard to parse in the moment.
  • Don’t assume a product is the same as last time. Manufacturers change recipes and suppliers, and a seasonal or regional item may use a different seasoning blend from the one you tried last trip.
  • Check the pack for a date or indication it’s a very recently introduced product — this matters specifically for cashew nut labelling, since stock already manufactured before the April 2026 requirement may lawfully omit it even though cashew is present.
  • If the allergen information is only in Japanese and you’re not confident, a store clerk can sometimes read it aloud or point to the relevant word, but they are not trained allergen advisors — they’re reading the same label you are.

What to say if you need to ask

Most konbini staff are stocking shelves and running registers, not trained in allergen consultation, so keep questions simple and point at the product. For packaged food, you’re really only confirming what’s printed, so a short opener works well:

すみません、食事制限があります。 (Sumimasen, shokuji seigen ga arimasu.) — “Excuse me, I have dietary restrictions.”

Followed by, if you need a direct yes/no on an ingredient:

小麦は使っていますか？ (Komugi wa tsukatte imasu ka?) — “Does this contain wheat?”

If you have coeliac disease and want to make the seriousness clear rather than sound like a casual preference, this line is worth having ready:

セリアック病のため、少量の小麦でも体調が悪くなります。 (Seriakku-byō no tame, shōryō no komugi demo taichō ga waruku narimasu.) — “I have coeliac disease, so even a small amount of wheat makes me ill.”

For hot-case items that aren’t individually labelled the way packaged shelf items are, it’s worth asking specifically about shared frying oil:

揚げ油はほかの料理と共用ですか？パン粉や衣のついたものと同じ油で揚げていますか？ (Age-abura wa hoka no ryōri to kyōyō desu ka? Panko ya koromo no tsuita mono to onaji abura de agete imasu ka?) — “Is the frying oil shared with other dishes? Is it fried in the same oil as breaded or battered food?”

Building a safer konbini shopping habit

Konbini shopping gets easier once you’ve identified a handful of product types that consistently work for you at a given chain — plain pickled plum onigiri with a clean ingredient list, or a salad with the dressing left aside, for example — but don’t let a good experience with one product make you skip the label on a similar-looking one next time. Recipes, suppliers and even seasonal packaging all shift.

If soy sauce is a recurring question mark for you across konbini, restaurants and izakaya alike, it’s worth reading in more depth: is soy sauce gluten-free in Japan? covers wheat-free brands, how to tell them apart, and when carrying your own bottle makes sense.

If you’re travelling with coeliac disease or a wheat allergy that’s severe enough to need medication on hand, carry it with you and follow your doctor’s guidance — this article is practical information, not medical advice.

Quick checklist

  • Packaged konbini food must declare wheat and the other mandatory allergens, but this covers intentional ingredients only — not shared-equipment cross-contact.
  • Barley isn’t a mandatory allergen in Japan, and macadamia/pistachio are only recommended labels, so check the full ingredient list if these matter to you.
  • Cashew nut labelling is only mandatory from April 2026, with older stock lawfully unlabelled until March 2028.
  • Treat soy sauce, miso, dressings and “wafū” seasoning as likely wheat sources, even on rice-based or vegetable-based products.
  • Hot case items (fryers, oden broth) usually sit outside the same packaged labelling and often involve shared equipment — ask directly.
  • Re-check the label every visit; recipes and suppliers change.
