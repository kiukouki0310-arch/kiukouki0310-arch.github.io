How to Explain Dietary Restrictions in Japan (Written Card)

October 20, 2026 · 7 min read

If you’ve ever tried to explain a dietary restriction in a busy izakaya or ramen counter using English, hand gestures, and a translation app, you already know the problem: your request often stops at the front-of-house staff and never reaches the person actually cooking. A written card in Japanese solves this because it can be handed to a chef directly, read carefully, and kept on the counter while your dish is being prepared - something a rushed verbal exchange rarely allows.

This guide is the one we point to from every other article on this site, because the method is the same whether you’re avoiding wheat, tracking down halal meat, eating vegan, or managing a serious allergy. Build your own card once, and you can reuse it at every meal in Japan.

Why speaking alone often isn’t enough

Japanese restaurant kitchens are usually organised in a clear hierarchy: a server takes your order, relays it verbally to the kitchen, and the kitchen acts on what they were told - not on what you actually said. If your request gets simplified along the way (“no egg” becomes “less egg,” or a wheat allergy becomes a vague dislike), nobody did anything maliciously wrong; the information just didn’t survive the handoff.

There’s also a language-literacy mismatch worth understanding. Many staff in tourist-facing restaurants can manage basic English menu terms, but detailed medical or dietary vocabulary is a different skill, and it’s unevenly distributed - a chain restaurant near a station may have more English support than a small family-run shop, which is often exactly the kind of place worth eating at. Speaking slowly or loudly doesn’t fix this; it’s a vocabulary gap, not a hearing one.

Finally, remember that Japan has no mandatory allergen labelling for restaurant food or anything sold loose over the counter - the 9 mandatory allergens (egg, milk, wheat, buckwheat, peanut, shrimp, crab, walnut, and from April 2026 cashew) only apply to prepackaged food. We cover this in detail in our guide to Japanese food allergy labels, but the short version is: at a restaurant, there is no label to check. The only information you’ll get is what staff tell you, which makes clear communication even more important.

Why a written card works better

A card fixes the handoff problem because it’s a physical object the server can carry straight into the kitchen and show the chef, rather than a paraphrased summary. It also removes pressure from the moment: you’re not trying to compose a sentence on the spot while a queue forms behind you, and staff aren’t trying to guess at an accent or a mispronounced word.

A good card does three things:

  1. States clearly that this is a dietary restriction, not a preference.
  2. Names the specific thing(s) you cannot eat.
  3. Gives staff an action - usually “please tell me what’s in this” or “can this be left out” - rather than just a list of problems.

It’s genuinely useful to show the card to kitchen staff directly if the restaurant allows it, not only the server. For anything involving cross-contact - shared frying oil, a shared grill, or shared cooking equipment - the person at the stove is the one who can actually answer.

Building your card: what to include

Your card should combine a handful of short, pre-written lines rather than one long paragraph. We’ve used only phrases checked by a native speaker, listed below exactly as they should appear - Japanese, romaji, and English together, so you can also read them aloud if you want to say something while you hand the card over.

The opening line (use for any restriction)

すみません、食事制限があります。 Sumimasen, shokuji seigen ga arimasu. “Excuse me, I have dietary restrictions.”

This signals seriousness before you’ve said anything specific, which helps staff switch into a more careful listening mode.

Naming the restriction

Pick the lines relevant to you:

Coeliac disease / wheat: 小麦が食べられません。 Komugi ga taberaremasen. “I can’t eat wheat.”

セリアック病のため、少量の小麦でも体調が悪くなります。 Seriakku-byō no tame, shōryō no komugi demo taichō ga waruku narimasu. “I have coeliac disease, so even a small amount of wheat makes me ill.”

Because regular soy sauce (koikuchi shōyu) is brewed with wheat, it’s worth adding: 醤油にも小麦が入っているので、醤油も食べられません。 Shōyu ni mo komugi ga haitte iru node, shōyu mo taberaremasen. “Soy sauce also contains wheat, so I can’t have soy sauce either.”

Soy sauce turns up in far more dishes than people expect - our breakdown of whether soy sauce is gluten-free in Japan is worth reading before you travel, since brands and wheat-free versions vary.

Vegan / vegetarian: 肉、魚、卵、乳製品が食べられません。 Niku, sakana, tamago, nyūseihin ga taberaremasen. “I can’t eat meat, fish, eggs or dairy.”

かつおだしや魚のだしも食べられません。 Katsuo-dashi ya sakana no dashi mo taberaremasen. “I can’t have bonito or fish stock either.”

Halal: 豚肉は食べられず、お酒も飲めません。 Butaniku wa taberarezu, osake mo nomemasen. “I can’t eat pork or drink alcohol.”

みりんや料理酒も使わないでください。 Mirin ya ryōrishu mo tsukawanaide kudasai. “Please don’t use mirin or cooking sake either.”

ポークエキスやラードも使わないでください。 Pōku ekisu ya rādo mo tsukawanaide kudasai. “Please don’t use pork extract or lard either.”

Hidden pork and alcohol derivatives are one of the biggest risks for halal diners in Japan - see our full guide to halal food in Japan for where they typically hide.

Food allergies: 〇〇のアレルギーがあります。 ___ no arerugī ga arimasu. “I’m allergic to ___.” Fill in the blank with the Japanese word for your allergen: 卵 tamago (egg), 乳 nyū (milk), 小麦 komugi (wheat), そば soba (buckwheat), 落花生 rakkasei (peanut), くるみ kurumi (walnut), えび ebi (shrimp), かに kani (crab).

If your allergy is severe: 重いアレルギーなので、調理器具の共用も心配です。 Omoi arerugī na node, chōri kigu no kyōyō mo shinpai desu. “My allergy is severe, so shared cooking equipment is also a concern.”

少量でも体調が悪くなります。 Shōryō demo taichō ga waruku narimasu. “Even a small amount makes me ill.”

The closing ask

End with a line that invites staff to respond with specifics, not just “yes”:

この料理に何が入っているか教えてください。 Kono ryōri ni nani ga haitte iru ka oshiete kudasai. “Please tell me what is in this dish.”

Or, if you want to check whether something can be adjusted: 抜いてもらうことはできますか？ Nuite morau koto wa dekimasu ka? “Could you leave it out?”

And once staff have answered and you’re satisfied: 大丈夫です、ありがとうございます。 Daijōbu desu, arigatō gozaimasu. “That’s fine, thank you.”

A worked example: a coeliac card

Put together, a card for coeliac disease might read, in order: the opening line, the coeliac line, the soy sauce line, and the closing ask about ingredients. That’s four short sentences - enough to be taken seriously without overwhelming the reader. If you want to ask specifically about frying oil (relevant for tempura, katsu, or tonkatsu-style dishes), add:

揚げ油はほかの料理と共用ですか？パン粉や衣のついたものと同じ油で揚げていますか？ Age-abura wa hoka no ryōri to kyōyō desu ka? Panko ya koromo no tsuita mono to onaji abura de agete imasu ka? “Is the frying oil shared with other dishes? Is it fried in the same oil as breaded or battered food?”

This matters for dishes you might not expect - even ramen broth and tare can contain wheat-based soy sauce, which is exactly why we have a dedicated breakdown of whether ramen is gluten-free.

Making the physical card

Format Pros Watch for
Printed card, laminated Durable, easy to hand over, works with no phone battery Bulky in a wallet
Photo saved on your phone Easy to carry, zoomable Needs battery/screen brightness in bright kitchens
Printed app/translation card Can be regenerated for new allergens Always double-check translation apps against the approved phrases above - machine translation can quietly drop “not” or swap an ingredient
Business-card sized version Fits in a wallet, feels less like a big interruption Smaller text - use a large, clear font

Whichever format you choose, make the text large and use a plain font. Staff often read these quickly on a busy floor, and small or stylised text works against you.

How to use the card in the moment

  • Hand it over early, ideally when you sit down or before ordering, not after food arrives.
  • Ask, where possible, for it to be shown to kitchen staff rather than kept at the front counter.
  • If staff seem uncertain or hesitate, treat that as useful information rather than a brush-off - it may genuinely be hard for that kitchen to accommodate you safely that day, and it’s fine to choose somewhere else.
  • Keep an eye out for 小麦 (komugi, wheat) and other allergen kanji on any printed allergy chart the restaurant hands you - many larger chains keep one behind the counter. You can ask: アレルギー表はありますか？ Arerugī-hyō wa arimasu ka? “Do you have an allergen chart?”

What a card can’t do

A card is a communication tool, not a guarantee. It cannot verify what actually happens in the kitchen, and it can’t eliminate cross-contact risk from shared grills, fryers, cutting boards, or utensils. Even with a clear card and a helpful, attentive member of staff, always confirm what you’re told rather than assuming, and ask again if a dish changes or a different person brings it to your table. If you carry rescue medication for a severe allergy, keep it with you at all times and follow your doctor’s guidance on when to use it - this article is a communication guide, not medical advice.

Quick checklist

  • Write your card using only the approved phrase combinations above, in order: opening line → specific restriction → closing ask.
  • Keep both a printed/laminated version and a phone photo as backup.
  • Hand the card over before ordering, and ask for it to be shown to the kitchen if possible.
  • Add the frying-oil question if you’re ordering anything fried.
  • Re-confirm with staff every time, even at a restaurant you’ve visited before - menus and suppliers change.
  • Carry any rescue medication with you and follow your doctor’s guidance for severe allergies.
