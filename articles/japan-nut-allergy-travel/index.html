Peanut & Tree Nut Allergies in Japan: Labels, Risks, Phrases

October 23, 2026 · 8 min read

If you’re travelling to Japan with a peanut or tree nut allergy, the question isn’t whether nuts appear in Japanese food — it’s where they turn up unexpectedly, and how much you can trust a label to warn you. The short answer: Japan’s allergen labelling system is genuinely useful for packaged food, but it has real gaps, and restaurants aren’t covered by it at all. Knowing exactly where those gaps are is the difference between a relaxed trip and a stressful one.

This guide covers how peanut and tree nut allergens are (and aren’t) labelled in Japan, which dishes commonly contain hidden nuts, and the specific Japanese you’ll need to explain a severe allergy to kitchen staff.

Why nut allergies need extra vigilance in Japan

Nuts aren’t a huge part of traditional Japanese cooking the way wheat or soy are, which can lull travellers into a false sense of security. But nuts show up in places that aren’t obvious to an outsider:

  • Wagashi (Japanese sweets) often use walnut or peanut in the filling or topping, even when the sweet looks plain from the outside.
  • Curry served at casual curry shops or added to commercial roux sometimes includes ground cashew or peanut paste for richness and thickening.
  • Machi-chuka (town Chinese) restaurants — the casual Japanese-Chinese diners found everywhere — frequently use peanut oil, peanut sauce, or crushed peanuts as a garnish on noodle and rice dishes.
  • Izakaya bar snacks often include a small dish of mixed nuts, which then sit on tables and get handled alongside shared plates.
  • Bakeries that bulk-bake nut breads and plain breads in the same space carry a high risk of cross-contact, even if the item you want doesn’t list nuts.
  • Breakfast buffets and cafés commonly offer granola, muesli, or nut-topped yogurt right next to “safe” options, with shared spoons.
  • Many Southeast Asian restaurants in Japan (Thai, Vietnamese, Indonesian) lean heavily on peanut in sauces like satay or gado-gado.

None of this means nuts are hidden on purpose — it’s simply that Japanese kitchens, like kitchens anywhere, use nuts as a flavour and texture ingredient in ways that aren’t always visible on a menu description.

How Japan’s allergen labelling actually works

Japan has a legally defined list of allergens that must be declared on packaged food sold in shops — but it’s shorter than you might expect, and it doesn’t cover everything.

Status Allergens
Mandatory (7 original) egg, milk, wheat, buckwheat, peanut, shrimp, crab
Mandatory since April 2025 walnut
Becoming mandatory April 2026 (transition until March 2028) cashew nut
Recommended only — not legally required macadamia, pistachio (and others such as sesame)

For nut allergies specifically, this means:

  • Peanut has long been a mandatory label — if a packaged product in Japan contains peanut, it should be declared.
  • Walnut only became mandatory in April 2025. Products manufactured or packaged before that date may still be on shelves without a walnut declaration, simply because they predate the rule.
  • Cashew is in a multi-year transition period. Until March 2028, manufacturers are allowed to use up older packaging that doesn’t mention cashew at all, even though it will soon be mandatory. Don’t assume “no cashew listed” means cashew-free during this window — check the ingredient list itself, not just the allergy box.
  • Macadamia and pistachio are only on the “recommended” list, meaning manufacturers are encouraged, but not required, to declare them. They can legally be present without appearing anywhere on the label.

The biggest gap: restaurants and loose food aren’t labelled at all

This is the part that catches travellers out. The mandatory labelling law applies to prepackaged food sold in stores — bagged snacks, boxed sweets, bottled sauces. It does not apply to:

  • Restaurant meals of any kind, from fine dining to fast food
  • Bakery items sold loose from a counter
  • Deli and prepared-food counters in supermarkets and department stores
  • Convenience store bento and rice balls made and packed in-store rather than centrally

In all of these settings, there is no legal requirement for anyone to tell you a dish contains nuts. Some larger chains voluntarily publish allergen charts for their menu, which is worth asking about directly — but plenty of independent restaurants, cafés and bakeries have nothing in writing. How to explain a nut allergy to staff

Japanese service culture takes customer requests seriously, but “I’m allergic to peanuts” needs to be said clearly and specifically — vague wording like “I don’t really like nuts” will be heard as a preference, not a medical issue.

Start with:

すみません、食事制限があります。 Sumimasen, shokuji seigen ga arimasu. “Excuse me, I have dietary restrictions.”

Then name the specific allergen using the fill-in-the-blank phrase, inserting 落花生 (rakkasei, peanut) or くるみ (kurumi, walnut) as needed:

〇〇のアレルギーがあります。 ___ no arerugī ga arimasu. “I’m allergic to ___.”

If your allergy is severe enough that shared equipment is a real risk — not just the ingredient itself but the fryer, the cutting board, the serving tongs — say so directly:

重いアレルギーなので、調理器具の共用も心配です。 Omoi arerugī na node, chōri kigu no kyōyō mo shinpai desu. “My allergy is severe, so shared cooking equipment is also a concern.”

It’s also worth asking whether a written allergen chart exists, since larger restaurants and chains increasingly keep one at the counter:

アレルギー表はありますか？ Arerugī-hyō wa arimasu ka? “Do you have an allergen chart?”

And to understand what’s actually in a specific dish before you order it:

この料理に何が入っているか教えてください。 Kono ryōri ni nani ga haitte iru ka oshiete kudasai. “Please tell me what is in this dish.”

If staff confirm a dish is fine, a simple acknowledgment keeps the exchange friendly and clear:

大丈夫です、ありがとうございます。 Daijōbu desu, arigatō gozaimasu. “That’s fine, thank you.”

For situations where speaking isn’t enough — noisy restaurants, language gaps, or when you want something in writing to hand over — our guide on explaining dietary restrictions with a written card covers how to prepare one that staff can read and act on.

A note on sesame

Sesame isn’t a tree nut botanically, and it isn’t one of Japan’s 9 mandatory allergens — it only sits on the “recommended” list alongside macadamia and pistachio. If you have a nut allergy and also react to sesame, be aware that sesame oil, sesame paste (goma), and sesame seeds are used extremely widely in Japanese cooking, from dressing to garnish to cooking oil blends, and may not be flagged on a menu or even on packaging. Treat sesame as a separate question to ask about, not something covered by asking about “nuts.”

Dishes and situations worth double-checking

Food / situation Why it’s a risk
Wagashi (Japanese sweets) Walnut, peanut or cashew sometimes used in fillings or toppings
Curry (shop or packaged roux) Ground nuts sometimes added for richness and thickening
Machi-chuka (casual Chinese) dishes Peanut oil, peanut sauce or crushed peanut garnish common
Bakery breads and pastries High cross-contact risk from shared ovens, trays and surfaces
Breakfast buffets / café granola Nuts often sit directly next to other items with shared serving utensils
Izakaya snack dishes Mixed nuts served as a side, handled alongside shared plates
Thai, Vietnamese, Indonesian restaurants Peanut-based sauces (satay, gado-gado) are a staple in many dishes
Fried food at shared fryers Oil and fryer baskets may be shared with nut-containing battered items

None of these dishes are automatically unsafe — many will be completely nut-free. The point is that you can’t tell from the name or appearance alone, so each one is worth a direct question rather than an assumption.

Building a routine for eating out

A reliable approach for severe allergies in Japan looks something like this:

  1. Before travelling, learn the kanji for peanut (落花生) and walnut (くるみ) so you can scan ingredient lists even without reading full sentences.
  2. At restaurants, lead with the allergy phrase and name the specific allergen rather than saying “nut allergy” generally, since staff may not know which nuts that covers.
  3. Ask specifically about shared fryers and cooking surfaces if your allergy is severe.
  4. For packaged food, check the ingredient list as well as the allergen box, especially for walnut (recently mandatory) and cashew (still in transition).
  5. When in doubt, choose simpler dishes with fewer components — plain grilled fish and rice is easier to verify than a multi-sauce dish.

Quick checklist

  • Peanut has been a mandatory label in Japan for years; walnut only since April 2025; cashew becomes mandatory in April 2026 with a transition period until March 2028 — check ingredients, not just the allergy box.
  • Macadamia and pistachio are only “recommended” labels, not required — they can be present without being declared.
  • Restaurant meals and loose counter food have no mandatory allergen labelling at all — always ask staff directly.
  • Watch for hidden nuts in wagashi, curry, machi-chuka dishes, bakery items, buffet granola, izakaya snacks and Southeast Asian restaurants.
  • Use the specific allergen phrase naming peanut or walnut, and mention shared cooking equipment if your allergy is severe.
  • Sesame is a separate concern from tree nuts and isn’t mandatorily labelled either — ask about it specifically if relevant.
  • Carry your prescribed allergy medication and follow your doctor’s guidance throughout your trip.
