Shellfish Allergy in Japan: Hidden Shrimp, Crab and Dashi Risks

October 24, 2026 · 6 min read

Why “shellfish allergy” is a trickier label in Japan than it sounds

“Shellfish allergy” is a catch-all term, but Japanese food law treats it as two separate things: ebi (えび, shrimp/prawn) and kani (かに, crab) are each individually one of the nine allergens that packaged food makers must declare. Everything else that English speakers might call “shellfish” — squid, octopus, scallops, clams, oysters, abalone — sits completely outside the mandatory labelling system. If your reactions extend beyond crustaceans to molluscs, that’s the single most important thing to understand before you eat anywhere in Japan.

On top of that, shrimp and crab turn up as flavouring agents in places that don’t look like seafood at all: stock, seasoning powders, sauces borrowed from Chinese-style Japanese cooking, and the shared fryer oil at a tempura counter. None of this means you can’t eat well here — it just means you need to ask specifically, every time, rather than trusting how a dish looks on the plate.

Ebi and kani: two separate mandatory allergens

Japan’s 9 mandatory allergens for packaged food are egg, milk, wheat, buckwheat, peanut, shrimp, crab, walnut (mandatory since April 2025) and cashew nut (mandatory from April 2026, with a transition period until March 2028 during which older stock may not yet show it). Barley is not mandatory, and macadamia and pistachio are only recommended, not required.

Because shrimp and crab are each listed separately, a product could theoretically contain one and not the other — always read both lines on the label rather than assuming “seafood” covers it. For a full walk-through of how to read these labels on konbini and supermarket packaging, see our guide to Japan’s mandatory allergen labels.

Remember too that this labelling law only applies to prepackaged food. Restaurant dishes, izakaya food, and anything sold loose over a counter — a bento made on-site, bakery items, department store food halls — have no legal requirement to list allergens at all. That’s exactly where asking staff directly becomes essential.

The gap most visitors miss: molluscs aren’t covered

This is worth repeating because it catches people out: squid (ika), octopus (tako), scallop (hotate), clam (asari, hamaguri), oyster (kaki) and abalone (awabi) are not among Japan’s mandatory allergens. A menu, a label, or even a staff member answering “no shellfish” may genuinely mean “no ebi or kani” while the dish still contains squid ink, octopus, or oyster extract.

If your allergy includes molluscs, you need to name the specific creatures you react to rather than relying on the general word “shellfish,” which doesn’t map cleanly onto how Japanese food culture, or Japanese food law, categorises things.

Where shrimp and crab actually hide

Shrimp and crab aren’t limited to the obvious dishes. Here’s where they turn up unexpectedly:

Hidden source Why it’s a risk
Dashi and soup stock Some ramen, Chinese-style Japanese soups, and regional dashi blends use dried shrimp or crab shells for depth, alongside or instead of the more common bonito/kombu base
Oyster sauce Common in yakisoba, chāhan (fried rice), gyoza dipping sauce and stir-fries at Chinese-Japanese restaurants; contains oyster extract, a mollusc
Kani kamaboko (crab-flavoured fish cake) Often made mostly from white fish surimi but flavoured with real crab extract; appears in salads, sandwiches, bento boxes and sushi rolls
Sakura ebi (dried baby shrimp) Mixed into okonomiyaki batter, takoyaki, rice crackers, furikake seasoning and some breads, sometimes without being obvious from the name
Shrimp chips and senbei Ebi senbei and prawn crackers are a classic snack, but shrimp also appears in less obviously named rice crackers
XO sauce and chili oil blends Popular in some ramen and noodle shops; frequently made with dried shrimp
Shared fryer oil Tempura counters and set-meal restaurants often fry shrimp tempura in the same oil as vegetables or chicken

Because of this, dishes that look entirely plant-based or meat-based can carry shellfish-derived seasoning. Dashi culture in particular means stock bases are rarely obvious from the menu description — our guide to why dashi is everywhere in Japan explains the same hidden-stock problem from a vegan angle, and the logic is identical here: you have to ask what the stock is actually made from.

Ordering safely: the phrases that matter

Start any order by flagging that this is a genuine dietary restriction, not a preference:

すみません、食事制限があります。 Sumimasen, shokuji seigen ga arimasu. “Excuse me, I have dietary restrictions.”

Then name the specific allergen using the fill-in-the-blank allergy phrase, swapping in the word for your allergen:

〇〇のアレルギーがあります。 ___ no arerugī ga arimasu. “I’m allergic to ___.”

For a shrimp allergy, fill the blank with えび (ebi). For a crab allergy, use かに (kani). If you react to both, say the phrase twice, once with each word.

Follow up by asking what’s actually in the dish, since menu names rarely list stock or sauce ingredients:

この料理に何が入っているか教えてください。 Kono ryōri ni nani ga haitte iru ka oshiete kudasai. “Please tell me what is in this dish.”

If a packaged product or restaurant has a written allergen reference, ask for it directly:

アレルギー表はありますか？ Arerugī-hyō wa arimasu ka? “Do you have an allergen chart?”

Many chain restaurants and some department store food halls keep an allergen chart behind the counter even if it isn’t posted — it’s always worth asking rather than assuming none exists.

If your reaction is serious, say so plainly, especially if you’re worried about shared woks, grills or fryer oil:

重いアレルギーなので、調理器具の共用も心配です。 Omoi arerugī na node, chōri kigu no kyōyō mo shinpai desu. “My allergy is severe, so shared cooking equipment is also a concern.”

If staff offer to remove the shrimp or crab garnish rather than remake the dish without it, you can ask:

抜いてもらうことはできますか？ Nuite morau koto wa dekimasu ka? “Could you leave it out?”

But treat “left out” with caution if your allergy is severe — a garnish removed at the last second doesn’t undo stock made with shrimp shells, or a sauce already stirred into a dish. If a kitchen can genuinely accommodate you, acknowledge it warmly:

大丈夫です、ありがとうございます。 Daijōbu desu, arigatō gozaimasu. “That’s fine, thank you.”

Because explaining a fairly specific allergy mid-conversation can be hard in a busy restaurant, it’s worth preparing something in writing in advance — our guide on explaining dietary restrictions with a written card covers how to set that up before you travel, which takes the pressure off getting every word right verbally.

Cross-contact: the part that’s easy to underestimate

Shared equipment is one of the most common ways a “shrimp-free” dish ends up with trace shellfish. Tempura counters frequently fry shrimp and vegetables in the same oil. Teppanyaki and okonomiyaki grills are often wiped rather than fully cleaned between orders. Woks at Chinese-Japanese restaurants move between dishes containing shrimp, squid and oyster sauce throughout a single service.

None of this means these places are unsafe by default — it means the risk depends entirely on how a specific kitchen operates that day, which only staff on-site can answer. If a restaurant can’t or won’t answer clearly about shared equipment, that’s useful information too, and it’s reasonable to choose somewhere else.

A note on severity

If your shellfish allergy is severe, always carry your prescribed medication with you and follow your doctor’s guidance on managing reactions while travelling — this article is practical food-safety information, not medical advice.

Quick checklist

  • Know that ebi (shrimp) and kani (crab) are separately mandatory on packaged labels — molluscs like squid, octopus, scallop and oyster are not covered at all
  • Name your specific allergen rather than saying “shellfish allergy” in English, since the category doesn’t translate cleanly
  • Ask what the dashi or stock is made from before assuming a dish is safe
  • Watch for oyster sauce in Chinese-Japanese dishes, sakura ebi in snacks and batters, and kani kamaboko in salads and bento
  • Ask about shared fryer oil, grills and woks if your reaction is severe
  • Carry your prescribed medication and follow your doctor’s guidance at all times
