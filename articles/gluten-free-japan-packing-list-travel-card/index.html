Gluten-Free Japan Packing List: What to Bring Beyond the Card

November 7, 2026 · 8 min read

If you’ve already made a dietary card in Japanese, you’ve done the most important thing. But a card handed to a server only works at the moment you hand it over — it doesn’t help you at 9pm when the restaurant is closed, it doesn’t explain a confusing convenience store label, and it doesn’t tell you what’s in the thing you can’t identify in your friend’s omiyage box. This list covers everything that sits around the card: the tools, snacks, and paperwork that make a gluten-free, allergy, vegan, or halal trip to Japan actually work day to day.

Japan is genuinely manageable for most dietary restrictions once you understand how the system works — but it rewards preparation more than most countries, because restaurant menus rarely list allergens, ingredient lists are sometimes abbreviated, and staff may not have handled a request like yours before. Packing well means you’re never relying on hope.

Why the card alone isn’t enough

A written card (see our guide on how to explain dietary restrictions in Japan if you haven’t made one yet) is excellent for communicating clearly with kitchen staff. But it assumes three things: that there’s a staff member free to read it, that the kitchen is flexible enough to change a dish, and that you’re in a sit-down situation at all. None of that applies to a train platform snack stand, a conbini at midnight, or a rural guesthouse with one elderly owner who speaks no English and has never seen a card like yours before.

That’s the gap this packing list fills.

Let’s go through the categories that need more explanation.

Translation tools: more useful than the card in daily life

The card is for restaurants. A camera-based translation app is for everything else — the back of a snack packet, a vending machine ticket menu, a handwritten specials board, a hotel breakfast buffet sign. Point your phone’s camera at Japanese text and most translation apps will overlay an English reading in real time, which is far faster than typing and far more reliable than guessing.

This matters especially for packaged food, because Japan’s allergen labelling rules only apply to pre-packaged products — the nine mandatory allergens are egg, milk, wheat, buckwheat, peanut, shrimp, crab, walnut (mandatory since April 2025), and cashew (becoming mandatory from April 2026, with a transition period until March 2028 during which older stock may still lack it). Barley is not mandatory, and macadamia and pistachio are only recommended, not required. Food sold loose over the counter — bakery items, deli counters, most restaurant dishes — has no mandatory allergen labelling at all. A translation app helps you read what is printed; it can’t tell you what isn’t there. Our guide to gluten-free Japanese snacks goes into which packaged categories tend to be lower-risk and which hide wheat in unexpected places.

Also save a short list of the kanji for your specific allergens so you can scan quickly without translating a whole label:

  • 卵 tamago — egg
  • 乳 nyū — milk
  • 小麦 komugi — wheat
  • そば soba — buckwheat
  • 落花生 rakkasei — peanut
  • えび ebi — shrimp
  • かに kani — crab
  • くるみ kurumi — walnut

If a label review in-app isn’t conclusive, staff are still your best resource. At a convenience store counter or small shop, you can ask directly: アレルギー表はありますか？ Arerugī-hyō wa arimasu ka? — “Do you have an allergen chart?”

Emergency snacks: pack more than you think you need

Pack three to five days’ worth of shelf-stable, safe snacks — not because Japan lacks suitable food, but because travel days are unpredictable. Shinkansen delays, early flights, a closed kitchen at a ryokan, a long transfer with nothing open: these are the moments emergency snacks exist for, not daily meals.

Good categories to bring from home or stock up on early in the trip:

  • Plain rice crackers or certified gluten-free crackers (if coeliac)
  • Nut-free energy bars (if you have a nut allergy, check ingredients carefully before travel — Japanese snack aisles contain plenty of options once you know how to read them)
  • Dried fruit or vacuum-packed fruit snacks
  • Vegan protein bars with a clear ingredient list you’ve already checked
  • For halal travellers, pre-checked packaged snacks without gelatin or alcohol-based flavourings

Restock as you go. Japanese convenience stores sell far more than onigiri — our guide to reading konbini labels explains which categories (plain rice, some fruit, certain dairy items) are generally easier to assess, and which (breaded items, many sauces, anything with a long seasoning list) need more scrutiny or a translation app in hand.

Medication and medical paperwork

This is not medical advice — always carry your prescribed medication and follow your doctor’s guidance for your specific condition, especially if you have a severe allergy.

Practical packing notes:

  • Split your medication across two bags. If you have an auto-injector or emergency medication, carry one set in your day bag and a backup in your main luggage — never pack all of it in one place that could be lost or delayed.
  • Carry a doctor’s letter, ideally with a Japanese translation, especially if you’re travelling with injectable medication, a large supply of tablets, or any specific medical food. Japanese customs generally permits personal-use medication in reasonable quantities, but a letter avoids confusion if you’re asked.
  • Keep medication accessible on your person, not in checked luggage or a locker you might not reach quickly.
  • If you have a severe allergy, it’s worth telling restaurant staff plainly rather than softening the request. The phrase 重いアレルギーなので、調理器具の共用も心配です (Omoi arerugī na node, chōri kigu no kyōyō mo shinpai desu) — “My allergy is severe, so shared cooking equipment is also a concern” — signals that this needs to be taken seriously, including cross-contact from shared pans, fryers, and utensils, not just the named ingredient itself.

Condiments worth packing from home

Regular Japanese soy sauce (koikuchi shōyu) almost always contains wheat, and it’s used far more widely than most travellers expect — in simmered dishes, dressings, marinades, and even some things that don’t taste overtly “soy saucy.” If you’re coeliac or avoiding wheat strictly, it’s worth packing a few travel sachets of wheat-free soy sauce (tamari-style) from home, since wheat-free versions are not universally available in restaurants.

At the table, you can ask: 持参した醤油を使ってもいいですか？ Jisan shita shōyu o tsukatte mo ii desu ka? — “May I use the soy sauce I brought?” Most staff are accommodating about this, especially if you’ve already explained your restriction. You can also check first with: 小麦不使用の醤油はありますか？ Komugi fushiyō no shōyu wa arimasu ka? — “Do you have wheat-free soy sauce?” For the full picture on where wheat hides in soy sauce and sauces built on it, see our explainer on soy sauce and gluten in Japan.

Phrases to have ready, not just written down

A card works best alongside a few spoken phrases you can say confidently, because it opens the conversation faster than handing over paper first. Starting with すみません、食事制限があります (Sumimasen, shokuji seigen ga arimasu) — “Excuse me, I have dietary restrictions” — before you even take out the card tends to get a more attentive response.

Once you have their attention, useful follow-ups include:

  • この料理に何が入っているか教えてください (Kono ryōri ni nani ga haitte iru ka oshiete kudasai) — “Please tell me what is in this dish.”
  • 抜いてもらうことはできますか？ (Nuite morau koto wa dekimasu ka?) — “Could you leave it out?”
  • 少量でも体調が悪くなります (Shōryō demo taichō ga waruku narimasu) — “Even a small amount makes me ill” — useful for emphasising that this isn’t a preference.
  • 大丈夫です、ありがとうございます (Daijōbu desu, arigatō gozaimasu) — “That’s fine, thank you” — to close politely once you’re satisfied with the answer.

For vegan or vegetarian travellers, 肉、魚、卵、乳製品が食べられません (Niku, sakana, tamago, nyūseihin ga taberaremasen) — “I can’t eat meat, fish, eggs or dairy” — is a clear opener, though dashi (often fish-based) needs a separate follow-up question since it’s rarely considered “meat” by kitchen staff.

For halal travellers, 豚肉は食べられず、お酒も飲めません (Butaniku wa taberarezu, osake wa nomemasen) — “I can’t eat pork or drink alcohol” — and ハラールのメニューはありますか？ (Harāru no menyū wa arimasu ka?) — “Do you have a halal menu?” — are good starting points before narrowing down to specific ingredients.

Quick checklist

  • Printed dietary card + photo backup on your phone
  • Translation app with camera/photo-scan function downloaded and tested before departure
  • Saved list of kanji for your specific allergens
  • 3–5 days of checked, shelf-stable emergency snacks
  • Travel-size wheat-free soy sauce (if coeliac or wheat-avoidant)
  • Prescribed medication split across two bags, with backup accessible on your person
  • Doctor’s letter (English + Japanese translation if possible) for medication or severe allergies
  • A few spoken phrases memorised, not just written down
  • A rough plan for where to restock snacks (convenience stores, supermarkets) once in Japan

Carry your medication as prescribed and follow your doctor’s guidance throughout the trip — this list supports good preparation, but it doesn’t replace medical advice for your specific condition.
