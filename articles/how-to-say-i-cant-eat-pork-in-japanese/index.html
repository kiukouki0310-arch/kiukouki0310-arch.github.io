How to Say 'I Can't Eat Pork' in Japanese: Halal Phrases

November 13, 2026 · 7 min read

If you need one sentence to start with, it’s this: 豚肉は食べられず、お酒も飲めません。 (Butaniku wa taberarezu, osake mo nomemasen.) — “I can’t eat pork or drink alcohol.” Say it slowly, hand over a printed card with the same words if you have one, and watch for a nod of understanding rather than a quick “hai, hai” that often just means “I heard you talking.”

That one sentence gets you most of the way, but pork in Japan rarely announces itself as a slice of meat on a plate. It turns up as lard in ramen broth, as flavouring powder in instant soup bases, and as an ingredient in sauces you’d never suspect. This article gives you the full set of phrases for pork, lard, mirin and cooking sake, explains why each one matters, and shows you how to put them together into a card you can hand to staff.

Why “no pork” isn’t enough on its own

In many countries, telling a server “no pork” is a complete instruction — the kitchen removes the pork chop or bacon and the rest of the dish is untouched. In Japan, pork and pork derivatives are often part of the base flavouring, not a visible ingredient you can simply lift off the plate.

Common places pork hides:

  • Tonkotsu ramen broth — built from pork bones, often for many hours. Even “clear” soy-based ramen broths are sometimes blended with pork stock.
  • Instant dashi and soup powders — some contain pork extract (ポークエキス, pōku ekisu) as a cheap flavour booster, including in miso soup packets and curry roux cubes.
  • Lard (ラード, rādo) — used for frying in some ramen shops, Chinese-style restaurants, and for cooking fried rice or gyoza.
  • Gyoza and nikuman fillings — pork is the default filling unless a menu specifically says otherwise.
  • Curry roux — many commercial roux blocks use beef or pork fat as a base, even in curries that look vegetable-only on the plate.

This is why the phrase list below includes lard and pork extract specifically, not just “pork.” A kitchen staff member who hears “no pork” might remove visible meat but not think to check the soup stock or the frying fat.

Alcohol hides just as often as pork does

Mirin (みりん) and cooking sake (料理酒, ryōrishu) are everywhere in Japanese home-style and restaurant cooking — simmered dishes (nimono), teriyaki-style sauces, some dressings, and many “shimmering glaze” finishes on grilled fish or chicken. Staff sometimes believe the alcohol “cooks off” completely during preparation, but this isn’t reliably true, and the amount that remains can vary by dish and cooking time. If this matters to your observance, it’s worth being specific rather than assuming a dish is alcohol-free just because it isn’t served as a drink.

The core phrases

Use these in order: start general, then get specific about pork, then specific about alcohol, then ask the direct question about a particular dish.

Japanese Romaji English
すみません、食事制限があります。 Sumimasen, shokuji seigen ga arimasu. “Excuse me, I have dietary restrictions.”
豚肉は食べられず、お酒も飲めません。 Butaniku wa taberarezu, osake mo nomemasen. “I can’t eat pork or drink alcohol.”
みりんや料理酒も使わないでください。 Mirin ya ryōrishu mo tsukawanaide kudasai. “Please don’t use mirin or cooking sake either.”
ポークエキスやラードも使わないでください。 Pōku ekisu ya rādo mo tsukawanaide kudasai. “Please don’t use pork extract or lard either.”
このお肉はハラールですか？ Kono oniku wa harāru desu ka? “Is this meat halal?”
ハラールのメニューはありますか？ Harāru no menyū wa arimasu ka? “Do you have a halal menu?”
この料理に何が入っているか教えてください。 Kono ryōri ni nani ga haitte iru ka oshiete kudasai. “Please tell me what is in this dish.”
抜いてもらうことはできますか？ Nuite morau koto wa dekimasu ka? “Could you leave it out?”
大丈夫です、ありがとうございます。 Daijōbu desu, arigatō gozaimasu. “That’s fine, thank you.”

A realistic exchange might go: open with the dietary restrictions line, follow with the pork-and-alcohol sentence, then add the mirin/cooking sake line and the pork extract/lard line so nothing gets missed. If the dish is a meat dish specifically, ask “Is this meat halal?” If you want to know if the restaurant has a halal menu at all, ask that question early, before you’ve picked anything from the regular menu.

If staff confirm something works for you, close politely with “That’s fine, thank you” — it signals the conversation is finished and leaves a good impression, which matters if you’re coming back or asking a follow-up question.

Why asking “is this halal?” sometimes isn’t enough either

“Halal” as a concept isn’t universally understood by restaurant staff in Japan, especially outside areas with established Muslim communities or international signage. Someone might answer “yes” meaning “there’s no pork meat in the ingredient list” without having checked the stock, the frying oil, or whether mirin went into the sauce. This isn’t dishonesty — it’s a gap in what the word “halal” is assumed to cover locally.

That’s why the phrase list breaks “no pork” into separate, concrete pieces: pork meat, pork extract, lard, mirin, cooking sake. Each one targets a specific ingredient category that a kitchen can actually check, rather than relying on staff to translate the single word “halal” into every relevant restriction themselves.

Shared equipment and cross-contact

Even when a dish itself is prepared without pork, mirin or sake, the same pan, grill plate, or simmering pot might be used for other dishes that do contain them. A grill that cooked pork belly ten minutes earlier and was only wiped down, not washed, still carries residue. A shared simmering pot used across multiple orders during dinner service may carry alcohol from an earlier dish into the broth of a later one.

If this level of carryover matters to you, it’s worth asking directly whether equipment is shared, and being specific about why — staff respond better to a concrete reason than a vague “I’m being careful.” You can combine this with the “please tell me what is in this dish” phrase from the table above, which opens the door to a fuller conversation rather than a yes/no answer.

Building a printable card

Carrying a written card in Japanese is one of the most effective tools for Muslim travellers in Japan, because it removes the pressure of real-time listening comprehension on both sides — staff can read it carefully, and you can point back to it if something’s unclear. A good card should include, in this order:

  1. The dietary restrictions opener (すみません、食事制限があります。)
  2. The core restriction sentence (豚肉は食べられず、お酒も飲めません。)
  3. The mirin/cooking sake line
  4. The pork extract/lard line
  5. A space to write or point at specific menu items, with the “what’s in this dish” question nearby

Keep the card simple — a phone screenshot works, but a printed card in a wallet or phone case survives low battery and poor wifi better. A note on severe allergies

This article is about dietary and religious restrictions rather than food allergies, but if you also manage a genuine food allergy alongside halal requirements, always carry your prescribed medication and follow your doctor’s guidance — the phrases here can help you communicate clearly, but they don’t replace medical precautions.

Quick checklist

  • Lead with すみません、食事制限があります。 (Sumimasen, shokuji seigen ga arimasu.) to signal this is a serious request, not a preference.
  • Use 豚肉は食べられず、お酒も飲めません。 (Butaniku wa taberarezu, osake mo nomemasen.) as your core sentence.
  • Add the mirin/cooking sake and pork extract/lard lines separately — don’t assume “no pork” covers them.
  • Ask “Is this meat halal?” or “Do you have a halal menu?” for a direct answer on specific dishes.
  • Watch for shared grills, fryers and simmering pots — ask about equipment if cross-contact matters to you.
  • Carry a printed card with these phrases in order; don’t rely on memory or a weak signal for translation apps.
  • Confirm with staff every single time, even at a place you’ve visited before — recipes and suppliers can change.
