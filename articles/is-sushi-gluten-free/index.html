Is Sushi Gluten-Free? Soy Sauce, Imitation Crab and Tamago Risks

October 25, 2026 · 8 min read

Short answer: it depends on what’s on top, not just the fish

Raw fish and rice sound like the safest meal in Japan for coeliacs — no noodles, no batter, no obvious bread. In reality, sushi is one of the easiest places to accidentally eat wheat, because so much of what makes sushi taste like sushi comes from soy sauce, soy-based glazes and processed ingredients rather than the fish itself.

The two biggest traps are soy sauce (regular koikuchi shoyu is brewed with wheat) and imitation crab (kani kamaboko, used in California rolls and some chirashi, usually contains wheat starch as a binder). Add tamago, eel sauce and the odd piece of tempura, and a sushi set can quietly contain wheat in four or five places before you’ve dipped anything.

This doesn’t mean sushi is off-limits — it means you need to look past “fish on rice” and check each component, every time, at every restaurant.

Soy sauce: the one that catches almost everyone

Standard Japanese soy sauce, koikuchi shoyu, is traditionally made from roughly equal parts soybeans and wheat. It’s not a trace contaminant — wheat is a core ingredient. This matters enormously at a sushi counter because soy sauce isn’t just the dipping sauce in the little dish; it’s often brushed directly onto nigiri by the chef, mixed into the tamago, used in the simmered sauces for things like anago (sea eel) or boiled shrimp, and blended into the glossy tsume (eel sauce) painted on unagi.

We’ve written a full breakdown of how shoyu is made and which versions are genuinely wheat-free in Is Soy Sauce Gluten-Free in Japan? What Coeliacs Need to Know — worth reading before any sushi trip, because the same ingredient shows up constantly.

At a sushi restaurant, assume the house soy sauce contains wheat unless you’re told otherwise. Some shops do stock a wheat-free (gluten-free) tamari-style shoyu, especially places used to serving overseas visitors, but it’s far from universal and even “tamari” varieties vary by brand — some still contain some wheat. Ask directly:

小麦不使用の醤油はありますか？ Komugi fushiyō no shōyu wa arimasu ka? “Do you have wheat-free soy sauce?”

If the answer is no, and you’ve brought your own bottle (a good habit for sushi trips), you can ask:

持参した醤油を使ってもいいですか？ Jisan shita shōyu o tsukatte mo ii desu ka? “May I use the soy sauce I brought?”

Crucially, even if you skip the dipping dish entirely, that doesn’t solve the problem — a lot of soy sauce exposure at a sushi counter happens before the plate reaches you, brushed on by the chef.

Imitation crab: wheat hiding in plain sight

Kani kamaboko — the pink-and-white “crab stick” used in California rolls, some gunkan-maki, and many chirashi bowls — is processed fish paste (surimi) that’s shaped and coloured to resemble crab. It’s a different product from genuine crab meat, and it’s a common source of hidden wheat: starch, often wheat starch, is frequently used as a binder and texturiser. Formulations vary by manufacturer, and not every brand uses wheat, but you can’t tell by looking or tasting, and restaurant kitchens won’t always know their supplier’s exact recipe off the top of their head.

Treat anything described as “crab roll,” “kani mayo,” or a cheap crab topping on chirashi as a likely wheat risk unless staff can confirm it’s genuine crab meat with no starch binder, or confirm the specific product. This is a good moment to use:

この料理に何が入っているか教えてください。 Kono ryōri ni nani ga haitte iru ka oshiete kudasai. “Please tell me what is in this dish.”

Real crab legs or whole crab meat sashimi don’t carry this particular risk, but they’re not always easy to distinguish from imitation crab on a menu translated into English, so it’s worth asking specifically rather than assuming.

Tamago: sweet, simple-looking, and often not wheat-free

Tamago-yaki, the slightly sweet folded omelette served as nigiri, looks like pure egg — and the egg itself is fine for coeliacs. The problem is what’s cooked into it. Traditional tamago is typically seasoned with a mix that can include soy sauce, mirin, and dashi, any of which may introduce wheat (soy sauce directly; some mirin and dashi products contain wheat-derived alcohol or seasoning depending on brand).

On top of that, tamago is often made in a shared rectangular pan, and at busy counters that pan — or the same cooking surface — may also be used for other seasoned items. It’s a low-risk item in terms of quantity, but not a safe assumption.

Eel sauce (tsume/nitsume): basically concentrated soy sauce

If you’re drawn to unagi (freshwater eel) or anago (sea eel) nigiri, know that the dark, sticky glaze brushed over the top — called tsume or nitsume — is a reduced sauce built from soy sauce, mirin and sugar, simmered down over time. It is, in effect, concentrated shoyu, and it is applied directly to the fish rather than served on the side, so there’s no way to “leave it off” once it’s plated. If you want eel, ask before it’s prepared:

抜いてもらうことはできますか？ Nuite morau koto wa dekimasu ka? “Could you leave it out?”

Be aware that removing a sauce that’s already soaked into the surface of grilled eel isn’t the same as never applying it — for anyone with coeliac disease, where even small amounts matter, it’s safer to choose a different topping rather than ask for sauce to be scraped off.

Sushi rice (shari) and the vinegar

The rice itself is seasoned with sushi vinegar — sushi-zu — a blend of rice vinegar, sugar and salt. Plain rice vinegar is naturally wheat-free, and this is one part of the meal that’s genuinely low-risk in most cases. That said, some pre-mixed commercial sushi vinegar blends include additional seasonings, and formulations differ between restaurant suppliers, so if you’re being very strict it’s reasonable to ask what the rice is seasoned with, especially at budget or chain restaurants using pre-made vinegar mixes rather than mixing their own.

Tempura, panko and shared frying oil

Some sushi sets and conveyor-belt menus include fried items — ebi tempura nigiri, fried chicken karaage as a side, or soft-shell crab rolls. These are coated in wheat-based batter or panko breadcrumbs, so the item itself is an easy “no.” The less obvious risk is the frying oil: if a kitchen fries battered shrimp and also fries, say, plain potato or vegetable sides in the same oil, wheat residue carries over. This is a bigger issue at sushi restaurants that also run a small fried-food menu than at pure nigiri specialists, but it’s worth asking rather than assuming, using the same phrase pattern used for tempura and katsu elsewhere in Japan.

Conveyor-belt sushi vs. counter sushi: different risk profiles

Kaiten-zushi (conveyor-belt sushi) and standard counter sushi carry different risks for coeliacs:

Conveyor-belt (kaiten-zushi) Counter / sit-down sushi
Sauces Often pre-applied at a central kitchen; hard to customise once plated Chef can often adjust in front of you if asked early
Communication High staff turnover, less time per customer, English may be limited More likely to have a chef who can answer detailed questions
Cross-contact Shared belt surfaces, high volume, pre-packaged toppings Shared knives/boards across fish types, but fewer wheat-containing items passing nearby
Best for Simple nigiri (plain fish, no sauce) ordered fresh rather than taken off the belt Asking detailed questions about soy sauce, tamago and specific toppings

If you’re using a conveyor belt, it’s generally more cautious to order fresh plates by touchscreen or verbally rather than pulling an already-sauced plate off the belt, since you can’t be sure what’s been brushed onto it or how long it’s been sitting near other dishes.

What to say at the sushi counter

Start by establishing that this is a serious dietary issue, not a preference:

すみません、食事制限があります。 Sumimasen, shokuji seigen ga arimasu. “Excuse me, I have dietary restrictions.”

セリアック病のため、少量の小麦でも体調が悪くなります。 Seriakku-byō no tame, shōryō no komugi demo taichō ga waruku narimasu. “I have coeliac disease, so even a small amount of wheat makes me ill.”

Then move into the specifics:

醤油にも小麦が入っているので、醤油も食べられません。 Shōyu ni mo komugi ga haitte iru node, shōyu mo taberaremasen. “Soy sauce also contains wheat, so I can’t have soy sauce either.”

この料理に小麦や醤油は入っていますか？ Kono ryōri ni komugi ya shōyu wa haitte imasu ka? “Does this dish contain wheat or soy sauce?”

If staff confirm something is free of wheat and soy sauce, you can close the exchange warmly:

大丈夫です、ありがとうございます。 Daijōbu desu, arigatō gozaimasu. “That’s fine, thank you.”

The safest order to build a sushi meal

  1. Plain sashimi-style nigiri on fish you can name (salmon, tuna, sea bream) — lowest risk once you’ve confirmed no sauce is brushed on.
  2. Ask specifically about tamago, eel/unagi, and anything described as crab, mayo-based, or tempura before ordering.
  3. Request soy sauce be left off or confirm a wheat-free version is available, rather than assuming you can just avoid the dipping dish.
  4. At conveyor-belt restaurants, order fresh rather than taking pre-sauced plates off the belt.

This is general food-safety guidance rather than medical advice; if you carry an epinephrine auto-injector or other emergency medication for a severe reaction, keep it with you and follow your own doctor’s guidance regardless of how careful the restaurant seems.

Quick checklist

  • Assume regular soy sauce (koikuchi shoyu) contains wheat, and ask about wheat-free alternatives before it touches your plate.
  • Treat imitation crab (kani kamaboko) as a likely wheat source unless confirmed otherwise.
  • Ask what’s seasoned into tamago before ordering it.
  • Remember eel/unagi sauce (tsume) is essentially reduced soy sauce, applied directly to the fish.
  • Rice vinegar (shari) is usually low-risk, but pre-mixed commercial blends can vary.
  • Avoid tempura and panko-coated items, and ask whether frying oil is shared.
  • On conveyor belts, order fresh plates rather than taking sauced items straight off the belt.
  • Use the approved phrases to explain coeliac disease clearly, not as a casual preference.
