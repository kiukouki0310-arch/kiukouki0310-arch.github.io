Peanut and Tree Nut Allergy in Japan: Labels, Risks & Phrases

November 11, 2026 · 7 min read

If you manage a peanut or tree nut allergy, the question that matters most in Japan isn’t “is this dish nutty” — it’s “does this product’s label actually have to mention nuts at all, and can I trust a restaurant to know what’s in the sauce.” The honest answer: packaged food labelling has improved a lot recently, but restaurant food has no mandatory allergen labelling at all, and nuts turn up in places you wouldn’t expect, from Chinese-style sauces to traditional sweets.

This guide covers what Japan’s label rules do and don’t cover for peanuts, walnuts and cashews, where nuts commonly hide, and the exact phrases to use so kitchen staff understand you’re describing a medical risk, not a preference.

What Japan’s allergen labelling actually requires

Japan has nine allergens that food manufacturers are legally required to declare on packaged food: egg, milk, wheat, buckwheat, peanut, shrimp, crab, walnut, and — from April 2026 — cashew nut. Walnut became mandatory in April 2025, so it’s now a reliable thing to look for on recent packaging. Cashew nut is newer: the rule starts in April 2026, but there’s a transition period running until March 2028, meaning older stock already on shelves may not show cashew on the label even after the rule technically applies. Don’t assume the absence of “カシューナッツ” on an older-looking package means it’s cashew-free — it may simply predate the new requirement.

Macadamia nut and pistachio sit in a different, weaker category: they’re only on Japan’s recommended labelling list, not the mandatory one. Manufacturers are encouraged to declare them but aren’t required to, so you genuinely cannot rely on the label either way for these two.

Here’s the quick reference:

Nut/allergen Status Since
Peanut (落花生, rakkasei) Mandatory Long-standing
Walnut (くるみ, kurumi) Mandatory April 2025
Cashew nut (カシューナッツ) Mandatory April 2026 (transition stock until March 2028)
Macadamia nut Recommended only —
Pistachio Recommended only —
Almond, hazelnut, pine nut, etc. Not covered —

Note that almonds, hazelnuts and several other tree nuts aren’t on either list. If your allergy extends beyond peanut, walnut and cashew, Japanese packaging simply won’t help you — you need to read ingredient lists for nut-adjacent words (nut pastes, nut oils, nut flours) and ask directly when eating out. Where peanuts and tree nuts actually hide

Japanese cooking doesn’t lean on nuts the way, say, Southeast Asian or Middle Eastern cooking does — which can lull people into a false sense of security. Nuts still show up, often in sauces or sweets where you wouldn’t think to look:

  • Chinese-influenced sauces and dressings. Some tantanmen (spicy sesame-based ramen) and bang bang chicken-style dressings use peanut as part of the flavour base, in addition to sesame. Peanut and sesame are often blended, so a “sesame sauce” label doesn’t rule out peanut.
  • Curry roux and some commercial curry sauces. A few brands add peanut or cashew for body and sweetness. This varies significantly by brand, so check the ingredient panel every time rather than assuming a curry you’ve had before is made the same way everywhere.
  • Wagashi (traditional sweets). Kurumi mochi and similar confections use walnut directly; some anko (sweet bean paste) varieties and senbei (rice crackers) are flavoured or topped with peanut or walnut.
  • Granola, cereal bars and baked goods. Walnut and cashew are common mix-ins in bakery items and convenience store snacks — always check the label rather than judging by appearance, since a plain-looking muffin can still contain chopped nuts baked into the batter.
  • Nut oils used for flavour, particularly in some dressings and finishing oils at higher-end restaurants, where a small amount of walnut or peanut oil can be used without it being obvious from the dish’s name.

If your allergy list overlaps with dairy or egg concerns too, it’s worth reading our guides on dairy hiding in bread, curry and sweets and where egg hides in food — nuts, dairy and egg often cluster in the same problem categories: sauces, baked goods and traditional sweets.

The restaurant gap: no mandatory labelling at all

This is the part that catches visitors out. Japan’s nine-allergen rule applies to packaged food sold in shops. Restaurant meals and food sold loose over a counter — a bakery pastry, a market snack, a bowl of ramen — have no mandatory allergen labelling whatsoever. Some larger chains voluntarily publish allergen charts, but many independent restaurants, ryokan kitchens and street stalls do not, and even where a chart exists it may not be current or may not cover every dish.

This means every restaurant meal needs a direct conversation, not a glance at a menu. Start by making clear this is a medical issue, not a preference:

すみません、食事制限があります。 Sumimasen, shokuji seigen ga arimasu. “Excuse me, I have dietary restrictions.”

Then name the specific allergen using the fill-in-the-blank phrase:

〇〇のアレルギーがあります。 ___ no arerugī ga arimasu. “I’m allergic to ___.”

Fill the blank with 落花生 (rakkasei) for peanut or くるみ (kurumi) for walnut — these are the two tree-nut-category words that are part of the approved fill-in list. If your allergy is to a nut outside that mandatory group (almond, hazelnut, cashew, macadamia, pistachio), say the English word clearly and, if possible, point to it written down — a printed allergy card is genuinely useful here, which is exactly what our printable Japanese allergy card guide is for.

Once staff know the allergen, ask what’s actually in the dish:

この料理に何が入っているか教えてください。 Kono ryōri ni nani ga haitte iru ka oshiete kudasai. “Please tell me what is in this dish.”

If the allergy is severe, say so plainly, and raise cross-contact — shared woks, shared cutting boards, and shared fryers are a real risk in Japanese kitchens, especially for anything stir-fried or deep-fried alongside nut-topped dishes:

重いアレルギーなので、調理器具の共用も心配です。 Omoi arerugī na node, chōri kigu no kyōyō mo shinpai desu. “My allergy is severe, so shared cooking equipment is also a concern.”

It’s also worth emphasising how much a small amount matters, since staff may assume a garnish-level sprinkle of walnut is negligible:

少量でも体調が悪くなります。 Shōryō demo taichō ga waruku narimasu. “Even a small amount makes me ill.”

If something can be removed — a nut garnish on a salad, a scattering of peanuts on noodles — ask directly:

抜いてもらうことはできますか？ Nuite morau koto wa dekimasu ka? “Could you leave it out?”

And if a larger restaurant or hotel keeps a written chart, it’s worth asking before you order:

アレルギー表はありますか？ Arerugī-hyō wa arimasu ka? “Do you have an allergen chart?”

When staff confirm something is fine, a simple acknowledgment keeps the exchange warm:

大丈夫です、ありがとうございます。 Daijōbu desu, arigatō gozaimasu. “That’s fine, thank you.”

A note on sesame vs. tree nuts

Sesame (goma) is extremely common in Japanese cooking — in dressings, tare, and as a garnish — and reactions to sesame are sometimes confused with tree nut reactions because the symptoms can overlap and sesame isn’t a tree nut botanically. Sesame is not one of Japan’s nine mandatory allergens, so it has no guaranteed labelling either. If sesame is a separate concern for you alongside peanuts or tree nuts, mention both by name to staff rather than assuming “no nuts” covers it.

Practical steps before you travel

  • Check the most recent packaging on anything you buy repeatedly — the April 2025 walnut rule and the 2026 cashew rule mean older stock in your cupboard at home may already differ from what’s on shelves in Japan now.
  • Don’t assume a product without カシューナッツ on the label is cashew-free until well past March 2028, since the transition period allows older labels to remain in circulation.
  • For convenience store shopping specifically, our guide to reading konbini labels for gluten explains the general layout of Japanese ingredient panels, which applies the same way to spotting nut allergens.
  • Carry a written card with your specific allergen(s) named in both English and Japanese, especially for nuts outside the mandatory list, where staff may not recognise the English word alone.
  • Always carry your prescribed allergy medication (such as an epinephrine auto-injector if prescribed) and follow your doctor’s guidance for your specific condition — this guide is practical travel information, not medical advice.

Quick checklist

  • Peanut and walnut are mandatory on Japanese packaged food labels; cashew nut joins them from April 2026, with older stock possibly unlabelled until March 2028.
  • Macadamia and pistachio are only “recommended,” not required — check ingredient lists yourself.
  • Almond, hazelnut and other tree nuts aren’t covered by Japan’s labelling rules at all.
  • Restaurant and loose counter food has no mandatory allergen labelling — always ask staff directly, every time.
  • Watch for nuts in Chinese-style sauces, curry roux, wagashi, and baked snacks.
  • Use the approved phrases above to name your allergen, ask what’s in a dish, flag cross-contact concerns, and confirm before eating.
  • Carry your medication and a written allergy card, and follow your doctor’s guidance for your specific allergy.
