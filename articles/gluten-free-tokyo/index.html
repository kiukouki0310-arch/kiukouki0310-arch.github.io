Gluten-Free Tokyo: How to Find and Order Food Safely

October 12, 2026 · 7 min read

If you have coeliac disease or another reason to avoid gluten strictly, Tokyo can feel like a city built against you: wheat-based soy sauce flavours almost everything, broths are rarely disclosed, and “gluten-free” as a labelling concept barely exists outside a small circle of specialist shops. The good news is that eating well here is absolutely possible — you just need to know where to look, what to ask, and which dishes carry hidden risk even when they look safe.

This guide focuses on strategy, not a list of restaurants (those change constantly and we can’t verify them for you). Instead, you’ll learn the types of places that tend to handle gluten-free requests well, the ingredients that cause the most problems, and the Japanese phrases that get your request understood rather than just smiled at.

Why gluten-free eating in Tokyo is harder than it looks

The core issue is that Japanese cooking relies on a small set of base seasonings that are used almost everywhere, and most of them contain wheat:

  • Koikuchi shoyu (regular soy sauce) is roughly half wheat by traditional production. It flavours sauces, marinades, dressings, simmered dishes, and the dipping sauces served with sushi, tempura, and soba. For a full breakdown of which soy sauces do and don’t contain wheat, see our guide on whether soy sauce is gluten-free in Japan.
  • Miso often contains barley or wheat koji depending on the type and brand, so it can’t be assumed gluten-free.
  • Dashi (the stock underlying most Japanese cooking) is sometimes made with pre-mixed seasoning packets that include wheat-derived ingredients, even in dishes that look like “just fish and vegetables.”
  • Tsuyu and ponzu, the sauces served with soba, tempura, and hotpot, are shoyu-based.
  • Wheat flour appears in tempura batter, katsu breading, okonomiyaki, takoyaki, gyoza wrappers, and many fried snacks.
  • Even soba (buckwheat noodles) is frequently made with a wheat-flour blend — pure 100% buckwheat soba (juwari soba) exists but is not the default.

None of this means gluten-free eating is impossible. It means you can’t rely on appearance or on dishes “sounding” safe (a grilled fish with vegetables can still be brushed with a wheat-based glaze). You need to ask, every time, even at places you’ve visited before — recipes, suppliers, and staff change.

Types of places, and how much you can realistically expect

Different categories of restaurant handle gluten-free requests with very different levels of understanding. Here’s a rough map to set expectations before you walk in.

Type of place What to expect Main risks
Specialist gluten-free cafés and bakeries Menus designed around the restriction; staff usually fluent in the topic Rare, often small, may need pre-booking
Vegan/vegetarian restaurants Staff are often used to detailed dietary questions and may know their suppliers well Soy sauce and miso in “vegan” dishes often still contain wheat — don’t assume vegan means gluten-free
Upscale kaiseki, sushi-ya, or ryokan dining Kitchens can often adapt with advance notice; chefs may substitute tamari or omit sauces Needs a phone call or email days ahead; last-minute walk-ins get less flexibility
Izakaya and casual local restaurants Huge menus, friendly staff, but gluten awareness is usually low Shared fryers, pre-made sauces, staff may say “no wheat” without checking seasoning packets
Chain family restaurants (teishoku, curry, ramen chains) Convenient, often have allergen charts online or at the counter Charts list wheat (小麦), but only as an ingredient — they don’t cover barley or cross-contact from shared fryers and prep areas, so still confirm with staff
Department store basement food halls (depachika) Wide variety, some prepared-food counters label ingredients Labels are usually Japanese-only, and food sold loose over the counter isn’t covered by mandatory allergen labelling — ask staff
Convenience stores Easy to buy, ingredient lists on packaging Wheat is extremely common even in savoury rice balls and snacks (soy sauce coating, soy sauce-flavoured seasoning)
International/Western restaurants (Italian, Western bakery-cafés) Some actively offer gluten-free pasta or bread Cross-contact in shared kitchens is common even when a GF option exists

A useful rule: the more a restaurant is used to foreign customers or dietary questions in general (vegan menus, allergy charts, English menus), the more likely staff can engage meaningfully with a gluten-free request. A completely local, Japanese-only izakaya might be delicious, but it is the hardest place to get a reliably verified answer.

What to actually ask — and the phrases that work

Vague English like “Is this gluten-free?” often doesn’t land, because “gluten-free” (グルテンフリー, guruten furī) is a known term but staff may not know which specific ingredients it rules out. It helps to be concrete about wheat specifically, and to ask about the sauce and broth, not just the main ingredient.

Useful phrases:

  • 小麦は使っていますか？ Komugi wa tsukatte imasu ka? “Does this contain wheat?”

  • 小麦不使用の醤油はありますか？ Komugi fushiyō no shōyu wa arimasu ka? “Do you have wheat-free soy sauce?” (Asking for “tamari” alone is not enough — some tamari brands still contain wheat. Ask to see the bottle and look for 小麦不使用 on the label.)

  • グルテンフリーの料理はありますか？ Guruten furī no ryōri wa arimasu ka? “Do you have any gluten-free dishes?”

  • セリアック病のため、少量の小麦でも体調が悪くなります。 Seriakku-byō no tame, shōryō no komugi demo taichō ga waruku narimasu. “I have coeliac disease, so even a small amount of wheat makes me ill.” (Coeliac disease is less familiar in Japan than wheat allergy, so stating that even a small amount matters tells staff how careful they need to be.)

  • 揚げ油はほかの料理と共用ですか？パン粉や衣のついたものと同じ油で揚げていますか？ Age-abura wa hoka no ryōri to kyōyō desu ka? Panko ya koromo no tsuita mono to onaji abura de agete imasu ka? “Is the frying oil shared with other dishes? Is it fried in the same oil as breaded or battered food?” This one matters more than people expect — a plain piece of fish fried in the same oil as a breaded cutlet can pick up gluten through cross-contact.

Writing your restriction down on a card (in Japanese) to hand to staff, rather than relying on spoken exchange in a noisy restaurant, often produces a more careful answer. Having a card ready before you sit down saves a confusing back-and-forth, and make sure its Japanese mentions soy sauce and other wheat-based seasonings, not just wheat flour.

Ingredients and dishes that deserve extra caution

A short list of things that look fine but frequently aren’t:

  • Dashi-based soups and simmered dishes (nimono) — the dashi packet may include wheat-derived flavour enhancers.
  • Teriyaki, yakitori sauce, and glazes — almost always soy sauce-based.
  • Miso soup — depends on the miso blend used; some are barley miso.
  • Soba — check specifically for juwari (100% buckwheat); standard soba is a wheat-buckwheat blend.
  • Okonomiyaki, takoyaki, gyoza — wheat flour is a core ingredient, not an add-on.
  • Curry roux — most Japanese curry bases use wheat flour as a thickener.
  • “Vegan” or “vegetarian” labelled dishes — these labels address animal products, not gluten; a vegan ramen can still have a wheat-based broth and wheat noodles.
  • Tempura batter and panko breading — obvious once you know, but easy to miss when a dish is described simply as “grilled” or “fried” without detail.

If your gluten intolerance comes with a severe allergic reaction risk rather than coeliac disease alone, carry your prescribed medication with you and follow your doctor’s guidance on managing accidental exposure — this guide is practical dining advice, not medical advice.

Planning ahead makes the biggest difference

Walking into a random restaurant and hoping for the best is the least reliable strategy in Tokyo. The highest-success approach looks more like this:

  1. Research before you go, rather than mid-meal. Look for restaurants that explicitly mention gluten-free, coeliac, or wheat-free options on their own website or official social media, in Japanese or English.
  2. Call or message ahead, especially for sit-down and higher-end restaurants. Many kitchens can adapt a dish with notice but can’t improvise safely on the spot during service.
  3. Ask your hotel concierge to help phone a restaurant and explain the restriction in detail — this is one of the most effective and underused tools, since the concierge can field follow-up questions in Japanese on your behalf.
  4. Confirm again at the table, even if you confirmed by phone or email. Staff shifts change, and the person who takes your reservation may not be the person who cooks or serves your food.
  5. Treat each visit as new. A dish that was fine last time may use a different sauce supplier this time, especially at smaller, independent restaurants.

Quick checklist

  • Assume regular soy sauce, miso, and dashi contain wheat unless a specific product is confirmed otherwise.
  • Don’t equate “vegan” or “vegetarian” with gluten-free — check sauces and broths separately.
  • Favour restaurant types used to detailed dietary questions: specialist gluten-free spots, vegan-focused restaurants, and upscale restaurants that take reservations.
  • Ask about shared frying oil and shared grills, not just the ingredient list of the dish itself.
  • Use a written Japanese dietary card or the phrases above rather than relying only on spoken English.
  • Call ahead or ask your hotel concierge to call for you, especially for anything beyond a casual meal.
  • Re-confirm at the table every visit, even at a restaurant you’ve eaten at before.
  • If you manage a severe allergy alongside gluten avoidance, carry your medication and follow your doctor’s guidance.
