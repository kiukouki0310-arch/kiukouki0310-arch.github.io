Shellfish Allergy in Japan: Where Shrimp and Crab Actually Hide

November 15, 2026 · 7 min read

If you have a shrimp or crab allergy, the worry isn’t really about ordering ebi tempura by mistake — it’s about the shrimp you can’t see. A splash of dried shrimp in an okonomiyaki batter, a stick of imitation crab tucked into a salad, a shared fryer that’s cooked shrimp all day. Japanese food uses small amounts of crustacean for flavour far more often than menus let on, and restaurant kitchens rarely list ingredients the way packaged food does.

The good news: once you know where shrimp and crab tend to hide, and how to ask about them, you can eat out with far more confidence. Here’s what to actually watch for.

Shellfish allergy vs. fish allergy — they’re not the same thing

This matters a lot in Japan, because the two get confused constantly.

Japan’s dashi (soup stock) culture is built mostly on fish — bonito flakes (katsuobushi) and dried sardines (iriko) — plus kelp (kombu). That’s a fish-based stock, not a crustacean one. If your allergy is specifically to shrimp and crab (crustaceans), bonito or sardine dashi is a different allergen category, though you should still ask, because some dashi blends and some shops do use dried shrimp or crab shell as part of their stock, especially in ramen, Chinese-style soups, and some seafood-forward dishes.

If your allergy covers fish as well as shellfish, you’ll want the broader picture on dashi — our guide to vegetarian dashi alternatives explains what dashi is typically made from and where kelp-only or vegetable versions exist, which is useful background even if you’re not vegetarian.

Where shrimp and crab actually turn up

Shrimp (えび ebi) and crab (かに kani) are two of Japan’s nine mandatory allergens for packaged food, which tells you something: regulators consider them common enough triggers to require labelling on products sold in packaging. But that mandatory labelling stops at the shop door — restaurant meals and food sold loose over the counter (bento counters, bakery shelves, street food stalls) have no legal requirement to disclose allergens at all.

With that gap in mind, here’s where crustacean turns up more than people expect:

  • Tempura and mixed fry — obviously risky if shrimp tempura is on the menu, but also worth asking about even for vegetable tempura, since it may be fried in oil shared with shrimp.
  • Ramen and Chinese-style noodle soups — some tonkotsu, miso and especially seafood-style broths use dried shrimp or crab shell for depth; shrimp wontons or shrimp gyoza are common toppings even in non-seafood bowls.
  • Okonomiyaki and takoyaki — tiny dried shrimp (sakuraebi) are a traditional ingredient in both batters, often not mentioned on the menu because it’s considered a standard seasoning rather than a topping.
  • Fried rice (chāhan) and gyoza — shrimp is a very common filling or mix-in in casual Chinese-Japanese restaurants.
  • Imitation crab (kani kamaboko) — the pink-and-white sticks used in sushi rolls, salads and sandwiches. Despite the name, some brands contain real crab extract or flavouring for taste, while others are entirely fish-based surimi with no crab at all. You cannot tell by looking, and it varies by manufacturer.
  • Rice crackers and snacks — shrimp-flavoured senbei (ebi sen) are everywhere in snack aisles and gift shops.
  • Sauces and seasonings — some oyster sauce, XO sauce, and certain curry or soup bases used in izakaya cooking contain shrimp or crab extract as a flavour base, even in dishes that otherwise look vegetable- or meat-based.

None of this means these dishes are automatically unsafe for you — it means they’re worth asking about every time, because the answer genuinely varies by shop and even by day’s batch.

What Japan’s allergen labels actually tell you

On packaged food in a supermarket or convenience store, shrimp and crab must be listed by law among the nine mandatory allergens (alongside egg, milk, wheat, buckwheat, peanut, walnut, and — from April 2026, with a transition period — cashew). That labelling is genuinely reliable for sealed products, assuming the label is current; walnut only became mandatory in April 2025, so very old stock might not reflect it yet.

What that labelling does not cover:

  • Restaurant meals, however fresh and however nice the kitchen
  • Hot food sold loose from a counter — bento boxes, bakery items, street stalls
  • Any dish where the fryer, grill, or stockpot is shared with shrimp- or crab-containing items

For the full picture of how the mandatory list works and its limits, our explainer on Japan’s food allergy labelling system goes into more detail on what’s required and what’s merely recommended (macadamia and pistachio, for instance, aren’t mandatory at all).

Cross-contact is the quiet risk

Even when a dish contains no shrimp or crab as an ingredient, shared equipment is a real concern for anyone with a serious crustacean allergy:

  • Fryers used for shrimp tempura are very often used for vegetable tempura and other fried items in the same basket-rotation
  • Teppan grills at okonomiyaki or yakisoba counters are wiped, not deep-cleaned, between orders
  • Ramen broth pots are large batch systems — a seafood-based tare (seasoning base) can sit in the same kitchen as a “plain” pork broth, with ladles and prep surfaces crossing over
  • Sushi counters handle raw shrimp, crab, and roe right next to other items on the same cutting boards

If your reactions are severe, it’s worth saying so explicitly rather than assuming staff will connect “allergy” with “please don’t let this touch other seafood.”

How to ask, in words that work

Japanese kitchen staff are generally careful and will take a clearly stated allergy seriously — but vague English like “I’m allergic to shellfish” doesn’t translate cleanly, and “no seafood” can be misread as a preference rather than a medical restriction. Be specific and direct.

Start with the basics:

すみません、食事制限があります。 Sumimasen, shokuji seigen ga arimasu. “Excuse me, I have dietary restrictions.”

To name the exact allergen, use this approved phrase and fill in the blank with the specific word for your allergen:

〇〇のアレルギーがあります。 ___ no arerugī ga arimasu. “I’m allergic to ___.”

For a shrimp allergy, fill the blank with えび (ebi) — so you’d say “Ebi no arerugī ga arimasu,” meaning “I’m allergic to shrimp.” For a crab allergy, fill it with かに (kani) — “Kani no arerugī ga arimasu,” meaning “I’m allergic to crab.” Both words are on Japan’s own mandatory allergen list, so staff at any restaurant that handles seafood will recognise them immediately.

If your reaction threshold is very low, add this so staff understand the stakes:

少量でも体調が悪くなります。 Shōryō demo taichō ga waruku narimasu. “Even a small amount makes me ill.”

For shared equipment worries, especially fryers and grills:

重いアレルギーなので、調理器具の共用も心配です。 Omoi arerugī na node, chōri kigu no kyōyō mo shinpai desu. “My allergy is severe, so shared cooking equipment is also a concern.”

To get real ingredient detail on an unfamiliar dish:

この料理に何が入っているか教えてください。 Kono ryōri ni nani ga haitte iru ka oshiete kudasai. “Please tell me what is in this dish.”

At chain restaurants and larger establishments, ask whether a printed allergen chart exists — many do, and it’s far more reliable than a verbal description under time pressure:

アレルギー表はありますか？ Arerugī-hyō wa arimasu ka? “Do you have an allergen chart?”

And if a dish can be adapted by removing a specific topping:

抜いてもらうことはできますか？ Nuite morau koto wa dekimasu ka? “Could you leave it out?”

When the answer satisfies you:

大丈夫です、ありがとうございます。 Daijōbu desu, arigatō gozaimasu. “That’s fine, thank you.”

Picking lower-risk places to eat

No restaurant category is risk-free, but some setups make it easier to get a clear answer:

Setting Why it helps or hurts
Chain restaurants with printed allergen charts Often have per-dish ingredient breakdowns verified by head office
Izakaya with an open kitchen You can see fryers and grills in use, and ask directly
Conveyor-belt (kaiten) sushi Higher cross-contact risk — shared belts, boards, and sauces touching many seafood items
Ramen shops using house-made broth Staff usually know the broth recipe well; ask about dried shrimp or crab in the tare
Convenience store bento and snacks Rely on mandatory packaging labels — check every time, as recipes change

For eating in more formal settings, the approach of being upfront early — before a multi-course meal starts, not partway through — applies just as much here as it does for other restrictions; see how it’s handled for a different allergen in our ryokan kaiseki guide for a sense of how advance notice helps kitchens prepare properly.

A note on severity

If your shellfish allergy carries any risk of a severe reaction, always carry your prescribed medication (such as an epinephrine auto-injector) with you and follow your doctor’s guidance on when to use it — this guide is practical travel information, not medical advice.

Quick checklist

  • State the specific allergen (shrimp/ebi, crab/kani) rather than a vague “shellfish” term
  • Ask what’s in unfamiliar dishes, especially ramen, okonomiyaki, fried rice and gyoza
  • Treat imitation crab sticks as unverified — ask whether the brand contains real crab extract
  • Ask about shared fryers and grills if your reaction threshold is low
  • Check packaged snacks for the shrimp/crab labels, but remember restaurant food has no such requirement
  • Request an allergen chart at chains and larger restaurants when available
  • Carry any prescribed emergency medication and know your own action plan
