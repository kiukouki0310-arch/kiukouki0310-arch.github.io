Shojin Ryori: Is Buddhist Temple Food Really Vegan in Japan?

October 30, 2026 · 7 min read

If you’ve read that Buddhist temple food in Japan is naturally vegan, you’re mostly right — but “mostly” is the word that matters. Shojin ryori (精進料理) is one of the few Japanese culinary traditions actually built around a plant-based philosophy rather than adapted to one. That makes it a genuinely good starting point for vegan travellers. It does not make every bowl labelled “shojin ryori” automatically safe for you, and knowing the difference is what this guide is for.

What shojin ryori actually is

Shojin ryori developed inside Zen Buddhist monasteries, where cooking itself was treated as a spiritual practice. The core precept is non-harm: no meat, no fish, no eggs, no dairy. Just as importantly for anyone trying to eat vegan in Japan, it also traditionally avoids fish-based dashi — the bonito (katsuo) stock that flavours an enormous amount of ordinary Japanese cooking. Instead, shojin cooks build flavour from kombu (kelp) and dried shiitake mushrooms, along with soy-based ingredients like tofu, yuba (tofu skin) and fermented miso.

A traditional meal is built around seasonal vegetables, rice, pickles, and soy proteins prepared with real skill to mimic the richness and texture that meat or fish would otherwise provide. It’s not a diet of plain boiled vegetables — well-made shojin ryori is considered a refined cuisine in its own right, served in multiple small courses similar in spirit to kaiseki.

The good news for vegan travellers

Because the tradition excludes meat, fish, eggs and dairy by design — not as a special request — shojin ryori is one of the only Japanese food categories where a full meal built this way isn’t unusual or difficult for the kitchen to produce. That’s a real advantage in a country where dashi made from bonito turns up almost everywhere, often invisibly, in miso soup, simmered vegetables, and sauces that look entirely plant-based.

There’s also a lesser-known detail that can matter to you: strict shojin ryori traditionally avoids the “five pungent roots” (gokun) — garlic, onion, scallion/green onion, leek and chives — because Buddhist practice considers them too stimulating. If you’re vegan for ethical or environmental reasons this won’t affect your safety, but it does mean a properly traditional meal will come without alliums, which some travellers with other sensitivities appreciate.

The catch: not all “shojin ryori” today is strictly traditional

This is the part worth slowing down for. “Shojin ryori” as a phrase on a menu doesn’t carry a legal or certified meaning the way, say, a packaged allergen label does. Over the past few decades the term has spread well beyond monastery kitchens into:

  • Temple lodging (shukubo) that serves genuine, strictly prepared shojin ryori to guests as part of a stay.
  • Temple-run or temple-adjacent restaurants, especially in tourist areas like Kyoto, where quality and strictness vary by establishment.
  • General restaurants and ryokan that borrow the name and visual style — multiple small lacquered dishes, tofu, vegetables — without necessarily following the dietary precepts. Some of these versions quietly use bonito dashi, or add a small amount of fish or egg because the kitchen is cooking for a general audience, not practising Buddhists.

In other words, the name signals an aesthetic as often as it signals ingredients. A beautifully presented set of vegetable dishes at a hotel breakfast calling itself “shojin-style” is not the same guarantee as a meal cooked in an actual monastery kitchen. Always treat the label as a starting point for a conversation, not as confirmation.

Wheat gluten (fu) is a staple here — a note for coeliac travellers

One ingredient shojin cooking relies on heavily is fu (麩), a wheat gluten product used to add chewy texture in place of meat. It’s a wonderful ingredient for vegan cooking and completely unsuitable for anyone avoiding wheat. If you’re travelling with both vegan and gluten-free needs, don’t assume a shojin meal solves both problems — fu, plus regular soy sauce (which also contains wheat), make most traditional shojin menus wheat-heavy by design. How to find the real thing

If you want an authentic shojin ryori experience rather than a vegetable-forward tasting menu with a similar name, a few approaches work well:

  1. Stay at a temple lodging (shukubo). Places like the temple lodgings on Mount Koya (Koyasan) or around Eiheiji are well known for serving shojin ryori as the standard evening meal for guests, prepared according to the monastery’s own kitchen tradition. Meals are usually included in the room rate and dietary needs can be flagged at the time of booking.
  2. Look for restaurants physically attached to or run by a temple, rather than ones simply using the name for atmosphere. Temple-run restaurants are more likely to follow the tradition closely, though quality and strictness still vary between temples.
  3. Check reviews and official temple or tourism association websites before travelling, since menus and strictness can change between visits and aren’t standardised across Japan.
  4. Book in advance wherever possible. Shojin ryori meals, especially at temple lodgings, are often prepared to order for a fixed number of guests, which is actually an advantage for you: it gives the kitchen a real chance to adjust the meal if you explain your restriction ahead of time, rather than improvising on the spot.

What to ask before and during the meal

Even in a kitchen built around plant-based cooking, you should still confirm details every time — the same rule that applies everywhere in Japan. Dashi is the biggest variable: some modern shojin-inspired restaurants quietly use a mixed or bonito-based stock for part of the menu even while most dishes stay vegetable-based.

Start the conversation clearly:

すみません、食事制限があります。 Sumimasen, shokuji seigen ga arimasu. “Excuse me, I have dietary restrictions.”

Then be specific about what you can’t eat:

肉、魚、卵、乳製品が食べられません。 Niku, sakana, tamago, nyūseihin ga taberaremasen. “I can’t eat meat, fish, eggs or dairy.”

かつおだしや魚のだしも食べられません。 Katsuo-dashi ya sakana no dashi mo taberaremasen. “I can’t have bonito or fish stock either.”

Ask directly about the stock, since this is where most hidden animal products hide in otherwise vegetable-looking dishes:

だしは何から取っていますか？ Dashi wa nani kara totte imasu ka? “What is the dashi (stock) made from?”

昆布だしや野菜だしの料理はありますか？ Konbu-dashi ya yasai-dashi no ryōri wa arimasu ka? “Do you have dishes made with kelp or vegetable stock?”

If you also avoid eggs and honey (common in vegan diets but not always obvious in Japanese sweets or glazes), add:

卵やはちみつも食べられません。 Tamago ya hachimitsu mo taberaremasen. “I can’t eat eggs or honey either.”

And once you’ve confirmed the details and you’re satisfied, a simple acknowledgment is appreciated and keeps the exchange friendly:

大丈夫です、ありがとうございます。 Daijōbu desu, arigatō gozaimasu. “That’s fine, thank you.”

Cross-contact and other practical considerations

Shojin ryori kitchens are generally lower-risk for accidental animal products than a typical izakaya or ramen shop, simply because so much of the menu starts from the same plant-based base. But “lower-risk” isn’t “risk-free”:

  • Some temple-adjacent restaurants cook shojin dishes in the same kitchen as a non-shojin menu for other guests, which can mean shared pots, grills or utensils.
  • Sweets and desserts served alongside a shojin meal (at a hotel or ryokan breakfast, for instance) may be sourced separately and aren’t guaranteed to follow the same rules — always ask about these separately.
  • If you have a diagnosed food allergy rather than a dietary choice, treat shojin ryori with the same caution as any other meal: confirm ingredients every time, and don’t assume “Buddhist” or “vegetable-based” means allergen-free. For background on how Japan’s allergen labelling works — and where it doesn’t apply, such as restaurant meals — see our guide to Japan’s dashi traditions and plant-based alternatives.

If you’re managing a severe allergy alongside a vegan diet, carry your prescribed medication with you and follow your doctor’s guidance, since no restaurant meal — however carefully described — can be guaranteed allergen-free.

Quick checklist

  • Treat “shojin ryori” as a tradition, not a certification — confirm ingredients at every restaurant, every time.
  • Temple lodgings (shukubo) and temple-run restaurants tend to follow the tradition most closely; general restaurants using the name may not.
  • Ask specifically what the dashi is made from — bonito stock is the most common hidden animal ingredient.
  • If you also need gluten-free food, be aware that fu (wheat gluten) and regular soy sauce are staples of shojin cooking.
  • Book ahead when possible so the kitchen has time to adjust the meal to your needs.
  • Carry any prescribed allergy medication and follow your doctor’s guidance regardless of how the meal is described.
