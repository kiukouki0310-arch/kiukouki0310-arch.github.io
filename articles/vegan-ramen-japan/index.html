Vegan Ramen in Japan: What's Hidden in the Bowl and How to Ask

October 21, 2026 · 7 min read

Ramen is one of the hardest dishes to eat vegan in Japan — not because vegan ramen doesn’t exist, but because a “plain” bowl of noodles in broth is almost never plain. The broth itself is usually the problem, and it’s invisible: a clear, savoury shoyu or shio ramen can be entirely built on bonito fish stock and pork bones, with nothing on the menu or in the bowl’s appearance giving that away.

The good news is that dedicated vegan ramen shops are genuinely growing in Tokyo, Kyoto, Osaka and other large cities, and some standard shops can adapt certain bowls if you ask the right questions before you order. This guide covers what’s actually in a typical bowl, which styles are easier to work with, and how to ask so staff understand you mean it.

Why ramen is rarely vegan by default

Ramen has several components, and almost every one of them can carry animal products.

The broth (supu). Most ramen broth is a combination of a base stock and a seasoning. The base is very often pork bones (tonkotsu), chicken bones, or a mix, simmered for hours. Even broths that look light and clear, like shio (salt) or shoyu (soy sauce) ramen, are frequently built on a combination of chicken or pork bones plus katsuobushi (dried bonito) or niboshi (dried small fish) for depth. A broth can be 100% meat- and fish-based even if it looks like clear vegetable soup.

The tare. This is the concentrated seasoning sauce ladled into the bowl before the broth is added — it’s often where fish sauce, dried fish, or meat extracts hide, independent of what the broth itself is made from. Two bowls that look identical can have completely different tare.

The oil (abura). Ramen almost always finishes with a spoonful of flavoured oil — often rendered pork fat (lard) or chicken fat — stirred through at the end for aroma and richness. This is easy to miss because it’s mixed into the broth, not sitting visibly on top.

Chāshū and toppings. Chāshū is braised pork (sometimes chicken), and it’s the default topping on most ramen styles. Ajitama (soft-boiled seasoned egg) is also standard. Menma (fermented bamboo shoot) and nori are usually plant-based, but menma can occasionally be simmered in a pork-based liquid, so it’s worth asking rather than assuming.

The noodles. Ramen noodles themselves are typically just wheat flour, water, salt and kansui (alkaline mineral water), which is plant-based. Some shops do use egg in their noodle dough, particularly for thicker or more yellow-toned noodles, so this is worth confirming too if egg is a concern for you, not only meat and fish.

If you’re also avoiding gluten, note that ramen noodles are wheat-based by definition, and the tare very often contains regular soy sauce, which itself contains wheat — a separate issue covered in detail in our guide to whether ramen can be gluten-free and our explainer on soy sauce and wheat in Japan.

Which ramen styles are more adaptable

Not all ramen is equally hard to adapt. Broadly:

  • Tonkotsu ramen — built entirely on pork bone broth, essentially never adaptable to vegan. Skip this style unless a shop specifically advertises a plant-based tonkotsu-style broth (some dedicated vegan ramen shops now make convincing versions from scratch).
  • Shio and shoyu ramen — the seasoning (salt or soy sauce) is just the surface layer; the underlying broth may still be meat- or fish-based. Some shops do offer a vegetable- or kelp-based version of these styles, but you cannot assume it from the name alone.
  • Miso ramen — miso paste itself is typically soybean-based and plant-based, but the broth it’s dissolved into is often pork- or fish-based, and some miso blends include bonito dashi. Ask rather than assume.
  • Tsukemen (dipping noodles) — the concentrated dipping broth has the same risks as regular broth, often more concentrated fish or pork flavour, so it needs the same questions.
  • Dedicated vegan or vegetarian ramen — a growing number of specialist shops, especially in larger cities, build broths from kelp (konbu), dried shiitake, soy milk, or vegetable stock. These are your most reliable option, but “vegetarian” on a sign doesn’t always mean strictly vegan — egg or dairy toppings can still appear, so confirm what’s actually in the bowl.

How to find vegan-friendly ramen shops

Because regular ramen shops have no obligation to list ingredients and staff may not expect the question, your best strategy is to look for places that market themselves specifically as vegan or vegetarian ramen, rather than hoping to adapt a traditional shop on the spot. Search in English and Japanese for “vegan ramen” plus the city name, check vegan-focused restaurant apps and directories, and look at restaurant websites or review sites (including photos of posted menus) before you go, since many dedicated vegan ramen shops do list ingredients or offer English menus. University towns and areas popular with international visitors tend to have more options.

What to say at the counter

Ramen shops are often fast-paced, with orders taken quickly at a counter or ticket machine, so it helps to speak up before you order rather than after the bowl arrives. Start by making clear this is a dietary restriction, not a preference:

すみません、食事制限があります。 Sumimasen, shokuji seigen ga arimasu. “Excuse me, I have dietary restrictions.”

Then state what you can’t eat:

肉、魚、卵、乳製品が食べられません。 Niku, sakana, tamago, nyūseihin ga taberaremasen. “I can’t eat meat, fish, eggs or dairy.”

Because bonito and fish stock are the single most common hidden ingredient in Japanese food, it’s worth naming it specifically:

かつおだしや魚のだしも食べられません。 Katsuo-dashi ya sakana no dashi mo taberaremasen. “I can’t have bonito or fish stock either.”

The single most useful question for any bowl of ramen, soup, or simmered dish in Japan is asking what the stock is made from, since “vegetable soup” and “fish-based soup” can look identical:

だしは何から取っていますか？ Dashi wa nani kara totte imasu ka? “What is the dashi (stock) made from?”

And to ask directly whether a plant-based version exists:

昆布だしや野菜だしの料理はありますか？ Konbu-dashi ya yasai-dashi no ryōri wa arimasu ka? “Do you have dishes made with kelp or vegetable stock?”

If a dish looks promising but you want full confirmation before ordering:

この料理に何が入っているか教えてください。 Kono ryōri ni nani ga haitte iru ka oshiete kudasai. “Please tell me what is in this dish.”

Cross-contact and shared equipment

Even at a shop that offers a vegetable-based broth, the same ladles, pots, and work surfaces may be used across meat, fish and plant-based orders, and broth is sometimes kept in large shared pots that are topped up throughout the day. If this matters for your situation — for a strict ethical vegan diet this is a personal line you’ll want to decide on consciously, and for coeliac disease, severe allergy or medical reasons it becomes a genuine safety issue — ask directly rather than assuming a “vegan” label on a menu means a fully separate preparation process. If you are managing a serious food allergy rather than a dietary preference, carry any prescribed emergency medication with you and follow your doctor’s guidance, since no restaurant staff can guarantee a dish is completely free of a specific allergen.

Quick checklist

  • Don’t trust appearance: a clear, light-coloured broth can still be built on fish or pork stock.
  • Ask what the dashi is made from before ordering, every time — it varies shop to shop and even bowl to bowl.
  • Tonkotsu ramen is essentially never vegan; shio and shoyu ramen need to be checked broth by broth.
  • Ask separately about the tare and the finishing oil — both are common hiding places for fish or meat.
  • Confirm whether noodles contain egg if that matters to you.
  • Look specifically for shops marketed as vegan or vegetarian ramen rather than hoping to adapt a traditional shop on the spot.
  • If cross-contact with shared pots or ladles matters to you, ask directly — don’t assume.
