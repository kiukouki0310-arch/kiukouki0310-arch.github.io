Vegan in Japan: Why Dashi Is Everywhere and How to Eat Well

October 17, 2026 · 7 min read

Yes, you can eat well in Japan as a vegan, but the challenge is not where most people expect. Meat is easy to spot. The real problem is dashi, a stock usually made from dried bonito fish, which forms the base of a huge share of Japanese cooking. A bowl of miso soup, a plate of simmered vegetables or a “vegetable” noodle dish can all contain fish without looking like it.

Once you understand dashi and a handful of other hidden ingredients, eating vegan in Japan becomes much more manageable. This guide explains where animal ingredients hide, which meals adapt best, and how to ask clearly.

This isn’t medical advice; if you’re also managing a medical condition, carry any prescribed medication and follow your doctor’s guidance.

Why dashi makes vegan eating hard

Dashi is the flavour foundation of Japanese cooking. The most common version combines katsuobushi (dried, smoked bonito flakes) with kombu (kelp). It is used in:

  • Miso soup and clear soups
  • Noodle broths for udon and soba, and their dipping sauces
  • Simmered dishes (nimono), including vegetable ones
  • Rolled omelette, savoury custards and many sauces
  • Instant seasoning powders used in home-style and casual restaurant cooking

Because dashi is so basic to the cuisine, staff may not think of it as “fish”. A vegetable dish is, to many cooks, simply a vegetable dish, even if the broth was made with bonito. That is why it helps to ask about the stock specifically, not just whether a dish contains meat or fish.

The good news is that kombu dashi (kelp-only stock) and mushroom-based stocks are traditional and widely understood. Many kitchens can make a version without bonito if they know in advance.

Where else animal ingredients hide

Beyond dashi, these are the most common surprises:

Ingredient Japanese Where it shows up
Bonito flakes かつお節 (katsuobushi) Sprinkled on chilled tofu, okonomiyaki, takoyaki, blanched greens
Small dried fish 煮干し (niboshi) / じゃこ (jako) Stocks, rice toppings, snacks
Chicken or pork extract エキス (ekisu) Sauces, seasonings, instant noodles, crackers
Lard ラード (rādo) Fried rice, gyoza, some breads and pastries
Egg 卵 (tamago) Tempura batter, mayonnaise, many noodles and sweets
Milk and butter 乳 (nyū) / バター (batā) Bread, curry roux, sweets, “matcha” drinks
Gelatin ゼラチン (zerachin) Desserts, gummies, some yoghurts
Honey はちみつ (hachimitsu) Sweets, dressings, drinks (many vegans avoid it)

Ramen is a special case. Even when the toppings are vegetables, the broth is usually pork, chicken or fish, and the seasoning (tare) often contains fish or meat extracts. Look specifically for shops that advertise a vegan broth rather than asking a regular shop to adapt.

Meals that adapt well

No category of food is automatically vegan, and recipes vary between restaurants. Still, some meals are much easier to adapt than others:

  • Shojin ryori (Buddhist temple cuisine). Traditionally made without meat or fish, using vegetables, tofu, sesame and kombu or mushroom stock. Temple restaurants and some temple lodgings serve it, often by reservation. Confirm that the stock is kombu or mushroom based, as modern adaptations can vary.
  • Tofu specialist restaurants. Common in Kyoto in particular. Many dishes are naturally plant-based, but dipping sauces may contain bonito, so ask.
  • Vegetable sushi. Cucumber rolls (kappa maki), pickled radish rolls and avocado rolls are simple and widely available. Check for mayonnaise, and ask about the rice seasoning and shared surfaces if you are strict.
  • Plain rice, edamame and grilled vegetables. Simple, but reliable building blocks when combined with other dishes.
  • Dedicated vegan and vegetarian restaurants. Larger cities have a growing number. Search in English and Japanese (ヴィーガン) and check recent reviews.

If you are staying at a ryokan with dinner included, contact the property well before you arrive. Traditional kaiseki dinners are built around dashi and seafood, but many inns can prepare a plant-based version with notice.

Shopping at convenience stores and supermarkets

Japan’s allergen labelling helps a little: packaged foods must declare egg (卵), milk (乳), shrimp (えび) and crab (かに). Fish and meat are not mandatory allergens, so you will need to read the full ingredient list (原材料名, genzairyō-mei) for words such as:

  • 魚介 (gyokai) — fish and seafood
  • かつお (katsuo) — bonito
  • 肉 (niku), 牛肉 (gyūniku), 鶏肉 (toriniku), 豚肉 (butaniku) — meat, beef, chicken, pork
  • エキス (ekisu) — extract (check what kind)
  • 動物性油脂 (dōbutsu-sei yushi) — animal fat
  • ゼラチン (zerachin) — gelatin

Useful options that are often plant-based, depending on the product: plain onigiri such as salted kelp (shio kombu) or pickled plum (umeboshi) fillings, roasted sweet potatoes, plain mochi, natto (but skip the sauce packet, which may contain bonito extract), fruit and nuts. Always check the label, because recipes change.

How to ask, in Japanese

Showing a request in writing works better than saying it aloud — a printed dietary card in Japanese is the easiest way. It removes pronunciation problems and lets staff show it to the kitchen.

  • 肉、魚、卵、乳製品が食べられません。 Niku, sakana, tamago, nyūseihin ga taberaremasen. “I can’t eat meat, fish, eggs or dairy.”
  • かつおだしや魚のだしも食べられません。 Katsuo-dashi ya sakana no dashi mo taberaremasen. “I can’t have bonito or fish stock either.”
  • だしは何から取っていますか？ Dashi wa nani kara totte imasu ka? “What is the dashi (stock) made from?”
  • 昆布だしや野菜だしの料理はありますか？ Konbu-dashi ya yasai-dashi no ryōri wa arimasu ka? “Do you have dishes made with kelp or vegetable stock?”
  • 抜いてもらうことはできますか？ Nuite morau koto wa dekimasu ka? “Could you leave it out?”

The second phrase is the most important one. Saying only that you do not eat meat or fish often leads to a dish made with bonito stock, because many cooks do not think of dashi as fish.

Practical tips for day-to-day eating

  • Eat a bigger breakfast or lunch at reliable places. Dinner options in small towns and late at night are more limited.
  • Use your hotel’s breakfast wisely. Western-style buffets often have bread, fruit and salad, but check bread for milk and egg.
  • Keep a few staples with you. Nuts, dried fruit and plain rice crackers are easy to carry. Check the label first, because many flavoured crackers contain shrimp, fish, bonito or milk.
  • Be specific and polite. Staff usually want to help; giving them a clear written list makes it much easier for them to say yes.

Plan ahead where you can

The most reliable vegan meals in Japan are the ones arranged before you arrive:

  • Ryokan and hotel dinners: email the property one to two weeks ahead.
  • Restaurant reservations: mention your diet when booking, not when you sit down.
  • Day trips: carry snacks, as rural areas and small towns have fewer options.

If you also avoid gluten, read Is Soy Sauce Gluten-Free in Japan?, because soy sauce and miso raise their own issues.

Quick checklist

  • Assume dashi contains fish unless staff confirm it is kombu or vegetable stock.
  • Watch for bonito flakes on tofu, greens and street food.
  • Remember ramen broth is almost never vegan unless the shop says so.
  • Look for shojin ryori, tofu restaurants and dedicated vegan places.
  • On packaged food, check for 卵, 乳, 魚介, かつお, エキス and ゼラチン.
  • Show written Japanese to staff, including the phrase about fish stock.
  • Recipes change, so confirm with staff every time.
