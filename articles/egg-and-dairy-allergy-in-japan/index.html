Egg and Dairy Allergy in Japan: Where Tamago and Milk Hide

November 17, 2026 · 6 min read

If you’re managing an egg or dairy allergy in Japan, the honest answer is that both ingredients turn up in far more dishes than their names suggest. Egg isn’t just in omelettes — it’s whisked into tempura batter, mayonnaise, and some noodle dough. Dairy isn’t just in bread and desserts — it’s a base ingredient in the curry roux blocks used in nearly every Japanese curry restaurant. Neither needs to be obvious on the plate to be present in the cooking.

This matters because egg and milk are two of Japan’s nine mandatory allergens for packaged food, but restaurant meals and food sold loose over the counter have no legal requirement to disclose allergens at all. That gap between “must be labelled” and “no label required” is where most accidental exposures happen.

Why egg and dairy are so easy to miss

Japanese cooking relies on a handful of workhorse ingredients that do a lot of invisible work. Egg is used as a binder in batters, a thickener in sauces, and a glaze on baked goods. Dairy shows up less as “cheese” or “milk” in the way Western menus flag it, and more as butter or milk powder folded into a roux, a dough, or a cream filling before it ever reaches the kitchen you’re eating in.

Because these are ingredients used in preparation rather than visible toppings, staff may not think to mention them unless you ask specifically. A cook who tells you a dish has “no milk” might be thinking of a glass of milk, not the butter in the sauce.

Egg: more than omelettes

Here’s where tamago (卵, egg) commonly turns up:

Dish or product How egg is used
Tempura (天ぷら) Egg is usually beaten into the batter
Mayonnaise Japanese mayonnaise is egg-yolk based and used generously as a garnish, in salads, on okonomiyaki, and inside many convenience store sandwiches and rice balls
Ramen and some noodles Fresh ramen noodles sometimes include egg in the dough; tsukemen and some udon-style noodles can too, depending on the maker
Okonomiyaki and takoyaki Egg is typically part of the batter
Karaage and other fried items Some batters use egg as a binder alongside flour
Bakery items Egg wash gives bread and pastries their golden shine
Western-style dishes Omelette rice (omurice), quiche, and katsu dishes coated in egg before breading

Dairy: curry, bread, and sweets

Milk and butter are less visually obvious than egg, which makes them easy to overlook:

  • Curry roux — the solid roux blocks used to make Japanese-style curry at home and in most curry restaurants typically contain milk powder and butter alongside wheat. Curry is often assumed to be a “safe” neutral dish, but it’s one of the higher-risk items for a dairy allergy.
  • Bread and bakery goods — Japanese milk bread (shokupan) and most sweet buns are built around butter and milk, not just as a glaze but as core dough ingredients.
  • Western-style sweets — cakes, soft serve, and cream-filled pastries are dairy-heavy by nature, but even items that look like they shouldn’t contain dairy (some curry breads, cream stew, white sauce pasta) often do.
  • Coffee and tea shop additions — creamers and some flavoured syrups can contain milk derivatives.

Reading labels: what’s required, what isn’t

Japan’s packaged food labelling law requires manufacturers to declare egg (卵) and milk (乳) if they’re present, because both are among the nine mandatory allergens — alongside wheat, buckwheat, peanut, shrimp, crab, walnut, and (from April 2026, with a transition period until March 2028) cashew. This means a bag of konbini snacks, a boxed bakery item, or a supermarket ready-meal should show egg or milk somewhere in the ingredient listing or a dedicated allergen note if either is present.

What this labelling rule does not cover:

  • Restaurant meals — no matter how formal the restaurant, there’s no legal requirement to list allergens on a menu.
  • Loose food sold over the counter — bakery items sold individually from a case, deli counter food, and most convenience store hot food (not pre-packaged) fall outside the labelling requirement.
  • Older stock during allergen transitions — because new allergen rules come with grace periods, products made before a rule change may not reflect the newest requirements.

What to actually say when ordering

Because menus won’t tell you, the conversation has to happen out loud — and it’s worth having the same conversation every time, even at a place you’ve visited before, since recipes and suppliers can change.

Start by signalling that this is a dietary restriction, not a preference:

すみません、食事制限があります。 Sumimasen, shokuji seigen ga arimasu. “Excuse me, I have dietary restrictions.”

Then name the specific allergen using this template, filling in the blank with the relevant word:

〇〇のアレルギーがあります。 ___ no arerugī ga arimasu. “I’m allergic to ___.”

For an egg allergy, insert 卵 (tamago). For a dairy allergy, insert 乳 (nyū). Staff are used to hearing this pattern filled in with a single ingredient word, so saying “tamago no arerugī ga arimasu” or “nyū no arerugī ga arimasu” out loud communicates clearly, even if you’re reading it off a card rather than speaking fluently.

Ask what’s actually in the dish, since names alone won’t tell you about the batter or the roux:

この料理に何が入っているか教えてください。 Kono ryōri ni nani ga haitte iru ka oshiete kudasai. “Please tell me what is in this dish.”

If the allergy is severe enough that shared equipment is a concern — for example, a fryer used for both tempura and other items, or a pan used for both curry and plain rice — say so directly:

重いアレルギーなので、調理器具の共用も心配です。 Omoi arerugī na node, chōri kigu no kyōyō mo shinpai desu. “My allergy is severe, so shared cooking equipment is also a concern.”

And at larger restaurants, chain izakaya, or hotel buffets, it’s worth asking outright whether they have anything written down:

アレルギー表はありますか？ Arerugī-hyō wa arimasu ka? “Do you have an allergen chart?”

Many chain restaurants and department store food halls do keep an allergen reference sheet in the kitchen, even if it isn’t displayed, and asking for it is a normal request staff are used to hearing.

Cross-contact: the part that’s easy to forget

Even a dish with no egg or dairy as listed ingredients can pick up traces from shared equipment. Common cross-contact points worth asking about:

  • Shared fryer oil — tempura, karaage, and tonkatsu may share the same fryer, and egg-based batter residue can linger in the oil even if your specific order has none.
  • Shared griddles — teppanyaki and okonomiyaki griddles are wiped, not deep-cleaned, between orders.
  • Shared sauce bottles and ladles — mayonnaise squeeze bottles and curry roux pots are often used across multiple dishes in a busy kitchen.
  • Bakery display cases — bread rolls sitting side by side on trays can transfer crumbs and glaze between egg-washed and non-egg-washed items.

None of this means the kitchen is being careless — it’s simply how fast-moving Japanese kitchens are set up, and most staff will adjust if you ask clearly and early, before the dish is prepared.

A note on severity

If your allergy is severe enough to risk anaphylaxis, always carry your prescribed medication (such as an epinephrine auto-injector) and follow your doctor’s guidance on when to use it, regardless of how carefully you’ve screened a meal. No restaurant staff explanation, label, or phrase card replaces that preparation.

Quick checklist

  • Treat mayonnaise as an egg product by default — it’s used far more widely in Japan than in most Western cuisines.
  • Assume curry roux contains dairy (and wheat) unless a restaurant tells you otherwise.
  • Remember that restaurant food and loose counter food have no mandatory allergen labelling — the spoken conversation is essential.
  • Check packaged food labels for 卵 (tamago, egg) and 乳 (nyū, milk) — both are mandatory allergens, but older stock during any transition period may lag behind the newest rules.
  • Ask about shared fryer oil, griddles, and sauce containers, not just the dish’s named ingredients.
  • Use the phrase for allergen charts (アレルギー表はありますか？) at larger or chain restaurants — many keep one even if it’s not displayed.
  • Carry any prescribed emergency medication and follow your doctor’s guidance, even when a dish seems low-risk.
