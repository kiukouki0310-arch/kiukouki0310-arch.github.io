Vegan Japanese Phrases for Dashi, Bonito, Egg and Honey

November 14, 2026 · 7 min read

If you’re vegan in Japan, the hardest ingredients to avoid aren’t the obvious ones. It’s dashi, bonito flakes, egg wash and honey — all things that can be stirred into a dish, brushed onto something, or simmered into a broth without ever appearing on a menu description. Saying “I’m vegan” in English often isn’t enough, because the word doesn’t map neatly onto how Japanese kitchens think about ingredients. You need a small set of specific, correctly-worded Japanese phrases, and a plan for when the answer isn’t what you hoped.

This article gives you those phrases — all checked against a native-speaker-approved list — and shows you how to turn them into a simple printable card.

Why “vegan” needs more explaining in Japan

In many countries, saying “I’m vegan” signals a whole category of exclusions at once. In Japan, the concept is less culturally established, and the word doesn’t automatically bring to mind fish stock. Dashi — the savoury stock base of miso soup, noodle broths, simmered vegetables, and countless sauces — is most commonly made from katsuobushi (dried bonito flakes) or niboshi (dried sardines). A dish can look completely plant-based — a bowl of simmered pumpkin, a vegetable tempura, a bowl of “vegetable” udon — and still be built on fish stock from the first step of cooking.

Egg and honey cause a different kind of confusion. Some vegans are comfortable with honey; many are not, and Japanese staff have no way of knowing which camp you’re in unless you say so. Egg shows up as a ramen topping, inside batter and binders, brushed onto bread as a glaze, and mixed into mayonnaise-based dressings that come standard on dishes you wouldn’t expect.

None of this means Japanese food is unfriendly to vegans — it means you need to ask the right questions, every time, rather than relying on how a dish is described. For the bigger picture on why dashi is so central to Japanese cooking, see our guide to eating vegan in Japan.

The core phrases to learn

These are the phrases that do the most work. Learn to say the first two confidently — they set the context for everything else.

Japanese Romaji English
すみません、食事制限があります。 Sumimasen, shokuji seigen ga arimasu. “Excuse me, I have dietary restrictions.”
肉、魚、卵、乳製品が食べられません。 Niku, sakana, tamago, nyūseihin ga taberaremasen. “I can’t eat meat, fish, eggs or dairy.”
かつおだしや魚のだしも食べられません。 Katsuo-dashi ya sakana no dashi mo taberaremasen. “I can’t have bonito or fish stock either.”
だしは何から取っていますか？ Dashi wa nani kara totte imasu ka? “What is the dashi (stock) made from?”
昆布だしや野菜だしの料理はありますか？ Konbu-dashi ya yasai-dashi no ryōri wa arimasu ka? “Do you have dishes made with kelp or vegetable stock?”
卵やはちみつも食べられません。 Tamago ya hachimitsu mo taberaremasen. “I can’t eat eggs or honey either.”
この料理に何が入っているか教えてください。 Kono ryōri ni nani ga haitte iru ka oshiete kudasai. “Please tell me what is in this dish.”

Say the first phrase to open the conversation and signal that this is a genuine dietary matter, not a passing preference — staff will take it more seriously and are more likely to check with the kitchen rather than guess. Then follow with whichever of the others fits the situation.

Asking about dashi and bonito specifically

Dashi is the single biggest risk for vegans in Japan, and it’s worth tackling head-on rather than hoping a dish is exempt. The phrase だしは何から取っていますか？ (Dashi wa nani kara totte imasu ka?) — “What is the dashi made from?” — is the most useful single question you can ask in any restaurant that serves soup, noodles, simmered dishes or many sauces.

Common answers you might hear back, in English or Japanese, include: katsuo-dashi (bonito), niboshi-dashi (dried sardine), awase-dashi (a blend, usually including fish), konbu-dashi (kelp only) and yasai-dashi (vegetable). Only the last two are typically plant-based, and even then, cross-contact is possible if the same pot or ladle is used for a fish-based stock elsewhere in service — it’s reasonable to ask about that too if your restriction is strict.

If the kitchen doesn’t have a plant-based stock on hand, follow up with 昆布だしや野菜だしの料理はありますか？ (Konbu-dashi ya yasai-dashi no ryōri wa arimasu ka?) — “Do you have dishes made with kelp or vegetable stock?” — to see if there’s an alternative dish rather than an alternative version of the same dish. For a deeper look at how dashi is built and where plant-based versions exist, our guide to dashi for vegetarians goes into more detail on sourcing and substitutions.

Ramen is a particularly common trap, since broths are frequently built on a bonito or combined fish-and-pork base even when the toppings look entirely vegetable-based. If ramen is on your itinerary, it’s worth reading about what’s typically hidden in the bowl before you order.

Egg and honey — the two that catch people off guard

Egg rarely announces itself. It appears as a soft-boiled topping on ramen, as a wash brushed onto baked goods, as a thickener in some sauces, and inside tamago-based items folded into sushi, bento boxes and sandwiches. Mayonnaise, which is egg-based in Japan just as it is elsewhere, is a default addition to many salads, okonomiyaki, and sandwich fillings — often without being listed as a separate ingredient on a menu.

Honey is less visible still. It can appear in dressings, in some breads and baked goods, and in drinks, without being an expected ingredient to a non-Japanese eye. Because not all plant-based eaters avoid honey, staff genuinely cannot guess your preference — you have to state it.

The phrase 卵やはちみつも食べられません。 (Tamago ya hachimitsu mo taberaremasen.) — “I can’t eat eggs or honey either” — is designed exactly for this: attach it to the end of your main statement so staff understand your restrictions cover more than the obvious meat-and-fish category.

Building a simple printable card

Spoken phrases are useful at the counter, but a printed card is more reliable in a noisy kitchen, with staff who are busy, or when you want to hand something over and let them read it carefully rather than parse spoken Japanese on the fly. A good vegan card combines three elements:

  1. The opener — すみません、食事制限があります。 (Sumimasen, shokuji seigen ga arimasu.)
  2. The restriction statement — 肉、魚、卵、乳製品が食べられません。 (Niku, sakana, tamago, nyūseihin ga taberaremasen.), with かつおだしや魚のだしも食べられません。 (Katsuo-dashi ya sakana no dashi mo taberaremasen.) and 卵やはちみつも食べられません。 (Tamago ya hachimitsu mo taberaremasen.) added as follow-up lines if those apply to you.
  3. The question — この料理に何が入っているか教えてください。 (Kono ryōri ni nani ga haitte iru ka oshiete kudasai.)

For the general principles of what a written card needs to say — font size, layout, where to show it, and how to handle a kitchen that can’t accommodate your request — see our guide on explaining dietary restrictions in Japan with a written card. The same structure works whether your restriction is vegan, gluten-free or allergy-based; only the specific phrases change.

When the answer isn’t what you wanted

Sometimes a dish simply can’t be made without dashi, egg or honey as currently prepared — that’s a normal outcome, not a failure of communication. Two phrases help you navigate this gracefully:

  • 抜いてもらうことはできますか？ (Nuite morau koto wa dekimasu ka?) — “Could you leave it out?” — useful when one specific ingredient, like an egg topping or a honey glaze, is the only issue.
  • 大丈夫です、ありがとうございます。 (Daijōbu desu, arigatō gozaimasu.) — “That’s fine, thank you.” — a polite way to close the conversation and move on if the kitchen can’t accommodate you, without creating an awkward standoff.

Staff in Japan generally want to help once they understand the request is serious, but busy kitchens — especially set-menu restaurants, izakaya and some ramen shops — may genuinely not have the flexibility to rebuild a dish on the spot. Knowing how to accept “no” politely keeps the interaction positive and makes it easier for the next traveller who asks.

Where this gets complicated: temples, konbini, and ramen

Shojin ryori, the Buddhist vegetarian cuisine served at some temples and temple-affiliated restaurants, looks like an obvious safe harbour — but even here, dashi can vary, and some versions use bonito despite the Buddhist framing. It’s worth asking the same dashi question even in a temple setting rather than assuming.

Convenience stores present a different challenge: packaged food in Japan must label the nine mandatory allergens (egg, milk, wheat, buckwheat, peanut, shrimp, crab, walnut, and cashew from April 2026), but there’s no requirement to flag honey, bonito or fish-derived dashi as such — you’ll need to read ingredient lists carefully rather than rely on an allergen box. Restaurant food and anything sold loose over a counter has no mandatory labelling at all, which is exactly why the spoken and written phrases above matter more in Japan than they might elsewhere.

If you also manage a food allergy alongside eating vegan, carry any prescribed medication such as an epinephrine auto-injector with you and follow your doctor’s guidance, since the phrases in this article are for communicating dietary choices and risk, not a substitute for medical advice.

Quick checklist

  • Open with すみません、食事制限があります。 to signal a genuine dietary matter.
  • State your restrictions clearly with 肉、魚、卵、乳製品が食べられません。, adding egg/honey or fish-stock lines as needed.
  • Ask だしは何から取っていますか？ before ordering any soup, noodle or simmered dish.
  • Ask 昆布だしや野菜だしの料理はありますか？ to find an alternative dish if the stock is fish-based.
  • Use 抜いてもらうことはできますか？ for single-ingredient fixes like an egg topping or honey glaze.
  • Keep a printed card as backup for busy or noisy kitchens.
  • Remember that packaged food labelling doesn’t cover honey or dashi source — read ingredients, don’t assume.
  • Close politely with 大丈夫です、ありがとうございます。 if a dish can’t be adapted, and choose something else.
