Printable Japanese Allergy Card: What It Must Say

November 8, 2026 · 7 min read

Why a translation app isn’t the same as an allergy card

If you’ve typed “I’m allergic to peanuts” into a translation app before a trip to Japan, you’ve probably had the uneasy feeling that the output doesn’t quite convey how serious this is. That instinct is correct. A good Japanese allergy card isn’t just a translated sentence — it’s a short, structured document that tells kitchen staff exactly what you can’t eat, how severe the reaction is, and what they should do about shared equipment and hidden ingredients.

The reason this matters more in Japan than in many other countries comes down to how kitchens work here. Fryers are often shared between breaded pork, shrimp tempura and vegetables. Dashi (the stock base for miso soup, simmered dishes and many sauces) is frequently made from bonito or other fish even when a dish looks plant-based. Regular soy sauce contains wheat. A card that simply says “no shrimp” doesn’t address any of that — it needs to prompt the right follow-up questions.

What a good allergy card must include

Think of the card as doing three jobs: naming the allergen precisely, communicating severity, and inviting the staff member to check specifics with you rather than guess.

1. A clear opening line that frames this as a health issue, not a preference. Starting with something like すみません、食事制限があります。 (Sumimasen, shokuji seigen ga arimasu. — “Excuse me, I have dietary restrictions.”) signals immediately that you need their attention before you order, not a quick nod mid-service.

2. The specific allergen(s), named individually — not grouped under “allergies.” Japan’s packaged food labelling system requires nine specific allergens to be listed on pre-packaged products: egg, milk, wheat, buckwheat, peanut, shrimp, crab, walnut (mandatory since April 2025) and cashew nut (mandatory from April 2026, with older stock possibly unlabelled during the transition period to March 2028). Barley is not a mandatory label item, and macadamia and pistachio are only recommended, not required. Restaurant menus and anything sold loose over a counter have no mandatory allergen labelling at all — which is exactly why your card needs to spell out the allergen by name rather than relying on a chart existing somewhere in the kitchen.

Use the approved pattern for naming an allergen directly: 〇〇のアレルギーがあります。 (___ no arerugī ga arimasu. — “I’m allergic to ___.”) filling in the blank with the specific word — for example 卵 (tamago, egg), 乳 (nyū, milk), 小麦 (komugi, wheat), そば (soba, buckwheat), 落花生 (rakkasei, peanut), くるみ (kurumi, walnut), えび (ebi, shrimp) or かに (kani, crab). If your allergen isn’t one of these eight, write the English name and, ideally, get a native speaker or a reputable translation service to add the Japanese word before you travel — don’t guess at kanji yourself.

3. A severity statement. This is the line that changes how carefully staff handle your order. 少量でも体調が悪くなります。 (Shōryō demo taichō ga waruku narimasu. — “Even a small amount makes me ill.”) tells them that trace amounts matter, not just a visible ingredient on the plate.

4. A note about shared equipment, if relevant. For more severe allergies, add 重いアレルギーなので、調理器具の共用も心配です。 (Omoi arerugī na node, chōri kigu no kyōyō mo shinpai desu. — “My allergy is severe, so shared cooking equipment is also a concern.”) This opens the door to asking about shared fryers, grills and cutting boards — genuine risks in many Japanese kitchens where frying oil in particular is often reused across dishes.

5. An invitation for staff to explain the dish or check an allergen chart. この料理に何が入っているか教えてください。 (Kono ryōri ni nani ga haitte iru ka oshiete kudasai. — “Please tell me what is in this dish.”) and アレルギー表はありますか？ (Arerugī-hyō wa arimasu ka? — “Do you have an allergen chart?”) give staff a concrete way to respond rather than just apologising and hoping for the best. Many chain restaurants and larger venues do keep an internal allergen reference binder, even if it’s not offered automatically.

6. A polite closing line for when you’re satisfied with the answer. 大丈夫です、ありがとうございます。 (Daijōbu desu, arigatō gozaimasu. — “That’s fine, thank you.”) closes the exchange warmly, which matters in service culture here and makes staff more willing to engage carefully with the next customer who hands them a card.

Free vs paid allergy card options

You’ll find a range of allergy card formats online, and the right one depends on how specific your needs are.

Option Good for Limitations
Free generic templates (PDF/image) with common allergens pre-translated Simple, well-known allergens like egg, milk, wheat, shellfish Often use stiff or outdated phrasing; may not cover severity or cross-contact
Build-your-own card using verified phrase lists Combining several allergens, adding your own severity notes Requires you to assemble it carefully and double-check wording
Paid translation services (per-card, done by a native speaker) Rare or multiple allergens, coeliac disease plus allergies, custom medical notes Costs money and takes a few days’ lead time
Hospital- or embassy-issued allergy documentation Backup for severe or complex medical situations Usually written for medical staff, not restaurant staff — pair it with a simpler card for everyday ordering

Whichever route you choose, the test is simple: read the Japanese back to a native speaker (or at least a second translation tool) before you travel, and confirm every phrase independently rather than trusting a single source. A card with confident-looking kanji that actually says something vague or wrong is worse than no card at all, because staff will trust it completely.

How to actually show the card to staff

The card only works if it’s used at the right moment, in the right way.

  • Hand it over before you order, not after. Ideally at the door or as soon as you’re seated, say すみません、食事制限があります。 (Sumimasen, shokuji seigen ga arimasu. — “Excuse me, I have dietary restrictions.”) and offer the card at the same time.
  • Expect them to take it to the kitchen. In many restaurants, the server who greets you isn’t the one cooking. Let them walk back with the card — this is normal and a good sign, not a brush-off.
  • Wait for a specific answer, not just a nod. A confident “daijōbu” (fine) from someone who hasn’t actually checked is a known risk. If the response feels vague, politely ask again or point to アレルギー表はありますか？ (Arerugī-hyō wa arimasu ka? — “Do you have an allergen chart?”) as a way to get something more concrete.
  • Use it at every single meal, even at places that seem casual or where staff speak some English. Hidden ingredients like dashi and soy sauce catch people out at simple-looking places as often as fancy ones.
  • Keep a photo of the card on your phone as backup in case the paper copy gets lost or you’re ordering somewhere without a counter to hand it across.

What the card can’t do

A card is a communication tool, not a safety guarantee. It can’t tell you whether a kitchen’s claim of “no cross-contact” is actually achievable on a busy night, and it can’t substitute for watching how food is handled or asking follow-up questions in person. No dish, product or restaurant should ever be described as “safe” outright — only as prepared a certain way, based on what staff tell you at that moment, which can change with the ingredients or the person cooking that day. If your allergy is severe, always carry your prescribed medication with you and follow your doctor’s guidance on managing reactions — a card supports communication, but it doesn’t replace medical preparedness.

Quick checklist

  • Name your specific allergen(s) individually on the card — don’t just write “allergies”
  • Include a severity line so trace amounts are taken seriously, not just visible pieces
  • Add a note about shared cooking equipment if your allergy is severe
  • Have the card checked by a native speaker or second source before you travel
  • Hand the card over before ordering, and wait for a specific answer, not just a nod
  • Keep a digital photo of the card as backup
  • Carry your prescribed medication at all times and follow your doctor’s guidance — the card helps you communicate, it doesn’t replace that preparation
