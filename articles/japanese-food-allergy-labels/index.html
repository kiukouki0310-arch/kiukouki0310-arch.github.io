Japanese Food Allergy Labels: The 9 Mandatory Allergens Explained

October 17, 2026 · 6 min read

If you have a food allergy and you’re standing in a convenience store or supermarket in Japan, the ingredient list can look like an unreadable wall of katakana and kanji. The good news is that Japan does have a labelling system for packaged food, and once you know the nine kanji to look for, you can scan a label in seconds. The less good news is that this system only covers packaged products — restaurant meals and food sold loose over the counter don’t have to disclose allergens at all, which is where most risk actually lives.

This guide walks through what’s legally required, what’s voluntary, what the label layout actually looks like, and how to ask staff directly when the packaging can’t help you. If you have a severe allergy, always carry your prescribed medication and follow your doctor’s guidance regardless of what any label says.

The 9 mandatory allergens (特定原材料)

Japan’s Food Labelling Act requires nine allergens — called tokutei genzairyō (特定原材料, “specified raw materials”) — to be declared on any packaged food sold in shops. Walnut was added by a 2023 revision and has been mandatory on all new products since April 2025. Cashew nut (カシューナッツ) was added from April 2026, bringing the total from eight to nine. Manufacturers have a transition period for cashew until March 2028, so packages made before then may still not declare it — always read the full ingredient list for カシューナッツ.

Allergen Kanji/script Romaji
Egg 卵 tamago
Milk 乳 nyū
Wheat 小麦 komugi
Buckwheat そば soba
Peanut 落花生 rakkasei
Shrimp えび ebi
Crab かに kani
Walnut くるみ kurumi
Cashew nut カシューナッツ kashū nattsu

These are the only allergens manufacturers are legally obliged to declare on packaging in Japan. If a product contains any of them, it must say so — usually either next to the relevant ingredient in brackets, or gathered into a short summary line near the end of the ingredient list.

A few things worth knowing:

  • Wheat (小麦) is mandatory; barley is not. This matters a lot for coeliac travellers, because Japanese labelling tells you about wheat but gives no information about barley, rye, or oats. For the full picture on this, see our guide on soy sauce and gluten in Japan.
  • Regular soy sauce (醤油, shōyu) almost always contains wheat. It’s one of the most common hidden sources of 小麦 in Japanese cooking, appearing in sauces, dressings, marinades and simmered dishes.
  • “Soba” restaurants often serve wheat noodles too. そば (soba/buckwheat) is a mandatory allergen in its own right — don’t assume a dish is buckwheat-only just because it’s called soba.

The “recommended” allergens (推奨表示)

Beyond the mandatory nine, there’s a second, longer list of allergens that manufacturers are encouraged but not required to label. These are sometimes called suishō hyōji (推奨表示, “recommended labelling”). Common items on this list include:

  • Soybean (大豆)
  • Sesame (ごま)
  • Beef, pork and chicken (牛肉, 豚肉, 鶏肉)
  • Salmon and mackerel (さけ, さば)
  • Squid and abalone (いか, あわび)
  • Salmon roe (いくら)
  • Kiwi, banana, peach, apple and orange
  • Macadamia nut and pistachio (pistachio was added to the recommended list in 2026)
  • Yam (やまいも)
  • Gelatin

Because this list is voluntary, coverage is inconsistent. A product might clearly flag sesame one week and say nothing about it the next, depending on the manufacturer’s own policy. Treat anything on the recommended list as “sometimes labelled, sometimes not” — useful when it’s there, but not something to rely on if it’s absent.

How to actually read the label

Japanese packaging generally follows one of two formats:

  1. In-line labelling — each ingredient that triggers a mandatory allergen has the allergen name in small brackets right after it. For example, an ingredient listed as a seasoning might be followed by a bracketed note naming 小麦 or 乳 as its source.
  2. Summary box — many products instead gather every mandatory allergen they contain into one line near the bottom of the ingredient panel, listing the kanji or katakana names together. This is often faster to scan than hunting through the full ingredient list.

Look out for these terms on the packaging:

Term Meaning
原材料名 Ingredient list
アレルギー物質 Allergen substances
特定原材料 Specified (mandatory) allergens
含む Contains

You may also see a separate disclaimer noting that the item was produced in a factory that also handles other allergens. This is a voluntary cross-contact warning, not a legal requirement, and its absence doesn’t mean the factory is allergen-free — it just means the manufacturer chose not to add that note. If you have a severe allergy, treat the lack of a cross-contact statement as “unknown” rather than “safe.”

What labelling does not cover

This is the part that catches visitors out. Japan’s allergen labelling law applies to prepackaged food sold in stores — bagged snacks, bottled drinks, boxed bentō, frozen meals. It does not apply to:

  • Restaurant and café meals, however simple
  • Bakery items sold loose by the piece
  • Deli and prepared-food counters (including supermarket and convenience store hot cases)
  • Food made to order, like a bowl of ramen or a plate of tempura

This means a convenience store’s packaged onigiri will have a full allergen summary on the wrapper, but the fresh bentō made that morning in the back of a small shop, or the curry at a restaurant down the street, legally doesn’t have to tell you anything. Staff knowledge varies enormously — some kitchens can rattle off every ingredient, others genuinely don’t know what’s in a sauce that comes premixed from a supplier.

Some larger restaurant chains do provide printed allergen charts voluntarily, usually covering the mandatory nine. It’s always worth asking:

  • アレルギー表はありますか？ / Arerugī-hyō wa arimasu ka? / “Do you have an allergen chart?”

If there’s no chart, go straight to the staff with a direct statement of your allergy rather than a vague mention of preference:

  • 〇〇のアレルギーがあります。 / ___ no arerugī ga arimasu. / “I’m allergic to ___.” (fill in with 卵 tamago, 乳 nyū, 小麦 komugi, そば soba, 落花生 rakkasei, くるみ kurumi, えび ebi, or かに kani)
  • この料理に何が入っているか教えてください。 / Kono ryōri ni nani ga haitte iru ka oshiete kudasai. / “Please tell me what is in this dish.”

For anything beyond a mild sensitivity, it helps to be explicit that this isn’t a dietary preference:

  • 重いアレルギーなので、調理器具の共用も心配です。 / Omoi arerugī na node, chōri kigu no kyōyō mo shinpai desu. / “My allergy is severe, so shared cooking equipment is also a concern.”

If you’re navigating Japan with wheat or gluten concerns specifically, our guide to ordering gluten-free in Tokyo covers phrasing and fried-food cross-contact in more detail, since shared frying oil is one of the biggest risks for coeliac travellers that labels alone can’t solve.

Konbini and supermarkets: your best friend for packaged food

Convenience stores (konbini) and supermarkets are genuinely useful for allergy management in Japan precisely because their packaged products carry mandatory labelling. Onigiri, sandwiches, instant noodles, snacks, and boxed desserts will all show the eight mandatory allergens if present. This makes konbini shelves a relatively low-uncertainty option compared with an unfamiliar restaurant kitchen — though you should still check every single item, since recipes and suppliers change often, and a product that was allergen-free last visit may not be this time.

If stock-based allergens matter to you (fish, for instance, though not on the mandatory list), it’s worth knowing how pervasive dashi is in Japanese cooking generally — our guide to dashi explains why it turns up in places you wouldn’t expect, which is useful background even for allergy readers, not just vegans.

Quick checklist

  • Learn the 9 mandatory kanji: 卵 (egg), 乳 (milk), 小麦 (wheat), そば (buckwheat), 落花生 (peanut), えび (shrimp), かに (crab), くるみ (walnut), カシューナッツ (cashew nut).
  • Remember these are only required on packaged food — restaurants and loose/counter food have no labelling obligation.
  • Treat the “recommended” allergens (soy, sesame, meats, fruits, nuts, gelatin and more) as inconsistently labelled, not guaranteed.
  • A missing cross-contact warning doesn’t mean a factory is allergen-free — it’s voluntary, not required.
  • At restaurants, ask アレルギー表はありますか？ and state your allergy directly with 〇〇のアレルギーがあります.
  • For severe allergies, add 重いアレルギーなので、調理器具の共用も心配です and carry your prescribed medication at all times.
  • Re-check every time — recipes, suppliers and kitchen practices change, even at places you’ve visited before.
