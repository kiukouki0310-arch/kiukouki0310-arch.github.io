Vegan Kyoto: Tofu, Yuba and Temple Food, Explained

November 4, 2026 · 7 min read

Kyoto is probably the easiest city in Japan to feel like you’re eating well as a vegan — tofu shops, yuba counters, and temples serving centuries-old Buddhist cuisine are everywhere. The catch is that a lot of that food isn’t automatically vegan just because it looks plant-based. The sauce poured over your silky tofu, the simmering liquid your vegetables sat in, even the “vegetable” soup at a temple — all of these can be built on bonito (katsuo) dashi, a fish stock that’s the backbone of most Japanese savoury cooking.

That doesn’t mean Kyoto is a trap. It means Kyoto rewards a bit of homework, because this is also the city with the strongest tradition of genuinely plant-based cooking in the whole country. Here’s how to find it, and how to check what you’re actually being served.

Why Kyoto is different from the rest of Japan

Kyoto was Japan’s capital for over a thousand years and home to hundreds of Buddhist temples, many of which still follow dietary precepts rooted in Zen Buddhism: no meat, no fish, and historically no strong-smelling vegetables like garlic or onion either. Out of that tradition came two things Kyoto is now famous for — shojin ryori (temple cuisine) and an unusually refined tofu and yuba culture, since temples needed protein-rich, plant-based ingredients that didn’t rely on animal products.

Tofu production in areas like Arashiyama and near Nanzenji developed specifically to serve these temples, using Kyoto’s soft water and local soybeans. Yuba — the skin that forms when soy milk is heated, skimmed off and often served fresh, simmered, or in delicate rolls — is a Kyoto specialty for the same reason. So the raw materials of a great vegan meal are genuinely everywhere here. The question is what gets added to them in the kitchen.

The shojin ryori trap

Modern shojin ryori restaurants exist on a spectrum. Some, usually attached to active temples or run by monks, still cook close to the original precepts — no meat, no fish, no dashi from animal sources, sometimes no garlic or onion either. Others are restaurants that borrow the aesthetic and the name but quietly use bonito dashi in the broth because that’s what gives Japanese food its familiar savoury depth, and most Japanese diners don’t expect shojin ryori to be strictly plant-based in practice.

We’ve covered this in detail in our guide to shojin ryori and whether it’s really vegan — the short version is: never assume. A beautifully arranged, clearly vegetable-based set menu can still have fish stock running through two or three of its small dishes. Ask directly rather than trusting the label “shojin ryori” alone.

Tofu restaurants: where the risk actually sits

Kyoto’s dedicated tofu restaurants — the kind serving yudofu (tofu simmered in a pot of hot water) as a multi-course meal — are some of the most plant-based-friendly dining in the city, but not automatically meat-free end to end. The tofu itself is simple: soybeans, water, and a coagulant. The risk sits in three places:

  • The dipping sauce. Yudofu is almost always served with a dipping sauce built on soy sauce and dashi, and that dashi is very often bonito-based.
  • Side dishes. A yudofu set usually comes with several small side dishes — simmered vegetables, a tempura piece, a pickle plate — and the simmered vegetables in particular are likely to be cooked in dashi too.
  • Garnishes. Bonito flakes (katsuobushi) are a common topping on otherwise vegetable-looking dishes, sprinkled on at the very end, so a dish can look completely plant-based and still have fish on it.

None of this means avoid tofu restaurants — it means ask about each component rather than assuming the whole set is dashi-free because the headline ingredient is soy.

Yuba: simple in theory, less simple on the plate

Fresh yuba on its own, served with just a little salt or soy sauce, is about as close to inherently vegan as Japanese food gets — it’s soy milk skin, nothing more. Where it gets complicated is in composed dishes: yuba simmered in a soup, yuba rolled around other ingredients, or yuba used in a kaiseki-style multi-course meal where the broths connecting each course are dashi-based. If you’re ordering yuba as part of a set course rather than on its own, treat it the same way you’d treat any other Kyoto specialty — ask what the surrounding liquid is made from.

The question that matters most: what’s the dashi made from?

If you only remember one phrase from this article, make it this one:

だしは何から取っていますか？ Dashi wa nani kara totte imasu ka? “What is the dashi (stock) made from?”

Dashi is the single biggest hidden risk in vegan eating anywhere in Japan, and Kyoto — with its soup-heavy kaiseki and shojin ryori traditions — has more of it per meal than most cities. Staff are used to being asked this, especially near temples and in restaurants that deal with tourists, and a good answer will usually be kombu (kelp), shiitake, or vegetable-based. If you want to ask more specifically whether an alternative exists, this follows naturally:

昆布だしや野菜だしの料理はありますか？ Konbu-dashi ya yasai-dashi no ryōri wa arimasu ka? “Do you have dishes made with kelp or vegetable stock?”

For a full breakdown of how dashi works and which types are typically plant-based, see our guide to vegetarian dashi in Japan — it’s worth reading before your trip so you recognise dashi mentioned on menus or by staff.

How to open the conversation

Start every order, whether at a tofu restaurant, a shojin ryori set menu, or a casual café, with a clear statement of what you can’t eat:

肉、魚、卵、乳製品が食べられません。 Niku, sakana, tamago, nyūseihin ga taberaremasen. “I can’t eat meat, fish, eggs or dairy.”

Then be specific about dashi, since “no fish” alone doesn’t always register as “no fish stock” to someone unfamiliar with how strict vegan eating needs to be:

かつおだしや魚のだしも食べられません。 Katsuo-dashi ya sakana no dashi mo taberaremasen. “I can’t have bonito or fish stock either.”

If you also avoid eggs or honey in confectionery — relevant if you’re tempted by Kyoto’s famous wagashi shops — add:

卵やはちみつも食べられません。 Tamago ya hachimitsu mo taberaremasen. “I can’t eat eggs or honey either.”

And for any dish you’re unsure about, this is the single most useful general-purpose line:

この料理に何が入っているか教えてください。 Kono ryōri ni nani ga haitte iru ka oshiete kudasai. “Please tell me what is in this dish.”

Where to actually look

Rather than naming specific restaurants (menus and ownership change, and we can’t verify current practice for you), here’s how to find the right type of place:

What to look for Why it helps
Restaurants attached to or near active temples, especially in Arashiyama, near Nanzenji, or in the Higashiyama area More likely to follow genuine shojin ryori precepts rather than using the name loosely
Dedicated vegan or “vegan-friendly” cafés (increasingly common around central Kyoto and near universities) Staff are specifically set up to answer dashi and ingredient questions, and menus often state preparation clearly
Tofu kaiseki restaurants that advertise “yudofu” as their specialty Strong plant-based base, but still confirm the dipping sauce and side dishes
Temple lodging (shukubo) that includes shojin ryori meals Often the most traditionally plant-based option, since meals are prepared for monks and guests under the same precepts — though it’s still worth confirming when booking
Buddhist temple gift shops and markets selling yuba to take home A good way to try plain yuba with no sauce ambiguity at all

Booking ahead and mentioning your dietary restrictions when you reserve — especially for kaiseki-style and temple lodging meals — gives the kitchen time to adjust, which is far more effective than asking once you’re already seated for a multi-course meal that’s mostly been prepped.

A note on severe allergies and intolerances

This guide focuses on vegan and vegetarian eating rather than allergies, but if you also manage a food allergy alongside your dietary choices, always carry your prescribed medication and follow your doctor’s guidance, since kitchen staff — however well-intentioned — cannot guarantee a completely allergen-free environment.

Quick checklist

  • Don’t assume “shojin ryori” means fully plant-based — ask about dashi specifically, every time.
  • At tofu restaurants, check the dipping sauce and side dishes separately from the tofu itself.
  • Use “Dashi wa nani kara totte imasu ka?” as your default question for any soup, simmered dish, or sauce.
  • Watch for bonito flakes as a garnish on dishes that otherwise look vegetable-only.
  • Book ahead for kaiseki or temple lodging meals and mention your dietary restrictions at reservation, not just on arrival.
  • Plain yuba and tofu bought to eat on their own are simpler to judge than composed, multi-course meals.
