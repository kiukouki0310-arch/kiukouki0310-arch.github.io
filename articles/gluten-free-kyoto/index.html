Gluten-Free Kyoto: Coeliac Guide to Kaiseki & Temple Food

October 18, 2026 · 8 min read

If you have coeliac disease and you’re heading to Kyoto, the question isn’t really “is Japanese food gluten-free” — it’s “how do I get through a multi-course kaiseki dinner or a temple’s shojin ryori lunch without eating wheat I can’t see.” Kyoto’s traditional cuisine is some of the most refined food in the world, and it is also some of the hardest to navigate gluten-free, because wheat shows up as a seasoning (soy sauce), as a thickener, and sometimes as a deliberate ingredient in its own right. The good news is that with some advance planning and a few key phrases, you can still experience it — just not by walking in unannounced.

This isn’t medical advice, and nothing here should be read as a guarantee that a specific dish or restaurant is safe for you. Always confirm with staff on the day, and if you carry emergency medication for a severe reaction, keep it on you at every meal.

Why Kyoto cuisine is particularly tricky for coeliacs

Two things make Kyoto different from, say, a casual udon shop in Osaka.

First, regular Japanese soy sauce (koikuchi shōyu) is brewed from wheat and soybeans, and it is used constantly — in simmering liquid (nimono), dipping sauces, glazes, and the seasoning base for countless small dishes. If a sauce tastes savoury and slightly sweet-salty, assume it contains wheat-based soy sauce until you’re told otherwise. We cover this in detail in Is Soy Sauce Gluten-Free in Japan?, but the short version for Kyoto is: you cannot avoid soy sauce here just by avoiding noodles.

Second, Kyoto’s refined kaiseki and temple traditions sometimes use wheat on purpose as a textural ingredient, not just a seasoning. The clearest example is 麩 (fu), wheat gluten, which is simmered, grilled, or shaped into decorative pieces — including the pretty, colourful “flower fu” you’ll see floating in clear soups at upscale restaurants. It looks like a vegetable or a sweet. It is almost pure wheat gluten. This is exactly the kind of hidden ingredient that makes restaurant dining in Japan risky for coeliacs: there is no mandatory allergen labelling on restaurant food or on food sold loose over the counter, unlike packaged products, where wheat is one of eight allergens manufacturers must declare. For a refresher on what packaged labelling does and doesn’t tell you, see Japanese Food Allergy Labels.

Kaiseki ryori: beautiful, multi-course, and built on dashi plus shōyu

Kaiseki is a sequence of small, seasonal courses — often eight to twelve of them — built around dashi (stock), soy sauce, miso, and seasonal produce. The structure itself isn’t the problem; the seasoning is.

Typical places wheat sneaks in:

  • Nimono (simmered dishes): the simmering liquid is almost always soy sauce-based.
  • Tsukuri/sashimi course: the fish itself may be fine, but the dipping soy sauce is not, and some restaurants brush a soy-based glaze onto the fish before serving.
  • Suimono/clear soup: often garnished with fu, as mentioned above, and the stock may include a splash of soy sauce or mirin-based seasoning.
  • Agemono (fried course): tempura batter is wheat flour, and even if a piece looks unbattered, the frying oil is frequently shared with breaded or battered items from earlier in the night.
  • Shime (final rice or noodle course): sometimes this is a wheat noodle dish, and even plain rice can be finished with a soy-based sauce.

Because kaiseki is cooked to a set menu prepared mostly in advance, this is not a cuisine where you can safely improvise table-side substitutions. The kitchen needs notice.

How to actually book kaiseki gluten-free

High-end kaiseki restaurants and ryokan dining rooms in Kyoto are generally used to dietary requests from international guests — more so than casual eateries — but they need lead time to redesign a course sequence.

  1. Contact the restaurant before you arrive, ideally when you book, not when you sit down. Many accept email or booking-platform messages in English; some only take phone calls in Japanese, in which case a hotel concierge or ryokan front desk can call on your behalf.
  2. Be specific, not just “gluten-free.” The word “gluten-free” (グルテンフリー, guruten furī) is understood by some higher-end kitchens but far from universal, and it doesn’t convey the seriousness of coeliac disease the way a clear statement about illness does.
  3. State the medical reality plainly. Use: セリアック病のため、少量の小麦でも体調が悪くなります。 / Seriakku-byō no tame, shōryō no komugi demo taichō ga waruku narimasu. / “I have coeliac disease, so even a small amount of wheat makes me ill.” This is far more useful to kitchen staff than a vague preference, because it tells them cross-contact matters, not just big portions.
  4. Ask about soy sauce specifically. 醤油にも小麦が入っているので、醤油も食べられません。 / Shōyu ni mo komugi ga haitte iru node, shōyu mo taberaremasen. / “Soy sauce also contains wheat, so I can’t have soy sauce either.” Many staff know wheat flour is a problem but don’t automatically connect it to shōyu.
  5. Ask whether a wheat-free soy sauce exists in the kitchen. 小麦不使用の醤油はありますか？ / Komugi fushiyō no shōyu wa arimasu ka? / “Do you have wheat-free soy sauce?” Some kitchens keep a tamari-style bottle for exactly this kind of request; many don’t, so this is worth asking well before your visit, not on the night.
  6. Consider bringing your own bottle of gluten-free soy sauce for situations where the kitchen can’t source one. 持参した醤油を使ってもいいですか？ / Jisan shita shōyu o tsukatte mo ii desu ka? / “May I use the soy sauce I brought?” A small travel bottle solves a surprising number of problems across an entire trip.

If a restaurant can’t commit to clear answers before your visit, that’s useful information too — it may be a sign the kitchen isn’t set up to rebuild a kaiseki sequence safely for you, and you’re better off choosing somewhere that can.

Shojin ryori: temple cuisine, vegan — but not automatically gluten-free

Kyoto’s Buddhist temples are famous for shojin ryori, the traditional vegetarian (in practice, largely vegan) temple cuisine that avoids meat, fish and often pungent vegetables like garlic and onion. It overlaps heavily with what we cover in our vegan dining guide for Japan, because both share the dashi problem — kelp (konbu) and shiitake stocks replace fish-based dashi, which is good news for vegans but says nothing about wheat.

Shojin ryori is not automatically gluten-free for two reasons:

  • It uses soy sauce and miso extensively as its main seasoning, since meat and fish-based flavour is off the table entirely.
  • Wheat gluten (fu) and seitan-style preparations are a celebrated part of the tradition — they’re often used specifically to create meat-like texture in a vegetarian meal, which makes them more, not less, likely to appear in a temple set menu than in an ordinary restaurant.

Temple shojin ryori meals almost always require advance reservation, sometimes days ahead, through the temple itself or a travel agent. That reservation step is actually an advantage for coeliac travellers: it’s your chance to flag dietary needs in writing before the kitchen has already decided the menu, rather than trying to renegotiate a set meal on arrival.

Dishes and ingredients to watch for around Kyoto

Kyoto item Typical gluten risk Why
麩 (fu), wheat gluten High Often pure wheat gluten, used in soups and simmered dishes
Yuba (tofu skin) Usually low, but confirm Made from soy milk, but may be served with a soy-sauce based dip or dashi containing soy sauce
Kyo-tofu / tofu dishes Variable Tofu itself is typically wheat-free, but the sauce, dashi or dipping soy sauce usually isn’t
Obanzai (Kyoto home-style side dishes) Variable Simmered in soy sauce-based liquid more often than not
Wagashi (traditional sweets) Variable Many use rice flour or starch and are fine; others use wheat flour (especially baked or fu-based sweets) — always ask
Matcha (the tea itself) Low Pure matcha powder and water are not a wheat risk, but some wagashi served alongside it is
Tempura (common alongside kaiseki) High Wheat-flour batter, plus shared frying oil

For any dish on this list — or anything that isn’t on it — the safest move is simply asking before you eat, every time, even at places you’ve been to before, because recipes and suppliers can change.

Phrases to carry through a Kyoto trip

A short, clear script works better than trying to explain coeliac disease from scratch at every counter. A practical sequence:

  1. Open with: すみません、食事制限があります。 / Sumimasen, shokuji seigen ga arimasu. / “Excuse me, I have dietary restrictions.”
  2. State the core restriction: 小麦が食べられません。 / Komugi ga taberaremasen. / “I can’t eat wheat.”
  3. Add the seriousness, especially anywhere food is cooked to order: 少量でも体調が悪くなります。 / Shōryō demo taichō ga waruku narimasu. / “Even a small amount makes me ill.”
  4. Ask about the specific dish: この料理に小麦や醤油は入っていますか？ / Kono ryōri ni komugi ya shōyu wa haitte imasu ka? / “Does this dish contain wheat or soy sauce?”
  5. If they offer to adjust something: 抜いてもらうことはできますか？ / Nuite morau koto wa dekimasu ka? / “Could you leave it out?”
  6. When you’re satisfied with the answer: 大丈夫です、ありがとうございます。 / Daijōbu desu, arigatō gozaimasu. / “That’s fine, thank you.”

Writing these phrases down on a card, alongside the kanji for wheat (小麦) and soy sauce (醤油), gives staff something to read carefully rather than trying to parse spoken English or Japanese under time pressure in a busy kitchen.

Where gluten-free dining is more realistic in Kyoto

If a formal kaiseki dinner feels too high-stakes for a first attempt, you have other options: contemporary restaurants in Kyoto that cater to overseas visitors sometimes offer a dedicated グルテンフリーの料理 (guruten furī no ryōri, “gluten-free dishes”) menu or section — ask directly with グルテンフリーの料理はありますか？ / Guruten furī no ryōri wa arimasu ka? / “Do you have any gluten-free dishes?” and treat any “yes” as a starting point for the detailed questions above, not the final answer. Hotel and ryokan concierges in the city are generally well practised at calling restaurants ahead for international guests, and it’s worth using that service rather than hoping an izakaya can improvise at 7pm on a Friday.

Quick checklist

  • Contact kaiseki restaurants, ryokan dining rooms, and temples offering shojin ryori well in advance — same-day requests rarely give the kitchen time to redesign a set menu.
  • Assume soy sauce is in almost everything savoury unless told otherwise; it’s wheat-based by default in Japan.
  • Watch specifically for 麩 (fu), wheat gluten, used decoratively in clear soups and simmered dishes — it’s easy to mistake for a vegetable.
  • Shojin ryori is typically meat- and fish-free, but not automatically wheat-free; confirm separately.
  • Carry a small bottle of gluten-free soy sauce for situations where the kitchen has none, and ask before using it.
  • Use the written phrase card approach rather than relying on a single spoken request.
  • For packaged snacks and wagashi bought to go, check the label for 小麦 (wheat) among Japan’s mandatory allergens, remembering that restaurant and loose counter food carries no such requirement.
  • Reconfirm with staff at every visit, even at places you’ve eaten safely before.
