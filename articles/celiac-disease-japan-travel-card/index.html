Coeliac Disease in Japan: Travel Card Wording That Actually Works

November 12, 2026 · 6 min read

If you’ve typed “celiac disease Japan travel card” into a search bar, you’ve probably already sensed the problem: a generic gluten-free card translated by an app often isn’t enough here. Staff may read it, smile, nod — and still bring you soy sauce-glazed chicken or tempura fried in shared oil, because the card didn’t explain why it matters or how much matters.

This isn’t about rude service. It’s about a gap in medical awareness. Coeliac disease is genuinely rare in Japan, and the word “gluten-free” (グルテンフリー, guruten furī) is often understood as a diet trend rather than a medical necessity. A card that works has to do more than list a restriction — it has to signal seriousness, explain the specific risk, and give staff something concrete they can check in their own kitchen.

Why coeliac disease is so little understood in Japan

Coeliac disease affects an estimated 1 in 100 people in parts of Europe and North America. In Japan, confirmed cases are far rarer, partly because wheat has historically played a smaller role in the traditional diet, and partly because awareness and testing are limited. Many restaurant staff have simply never served a coeliac guest and have no mental model for what “a little bit of wheat” can do.

This matters practically because:

  • Wheat gluten and wheat-contaminated soy sauce aren’t seen as a medical issue by default. Regular koikuchi soy sauce is brewed from wheat and soybeans, and it’s everywhere — in broths, dressings, marinades, and glazes that don’t look “wheat-like” at all. For more on this specific risk, see our guide to soy sauce and gluten in Japan.
  • “Gluten-free” is sometimes read as a lifestyle choice. Staff may assume a small amount is fine, or that avoiding obvious bread and noodles is enough, without realising that sauces, dashi bases, and batters can hide wheat too.
  • Kitchens are small and shared. Fryers, grills and cutting boards are frequently used for multiple dishes in sequence, so cross-contact is a real and ongoing risk even when the dish itself is “gluten-free” on paper.
  • Packaged food labelling doesn’t extend to restaurants. Japan’s 9 mandatory allergen labels (egg, milk, wheat, buckwheat, peanut, shrimp, crab, walnut and — from April 2026, with a transition period to March 2028 — cashew) apply to packaged products, not to restaurant menus or food sold loose at a counter. That means there’s no legal requirement for a kitchen to tell you about wheat in a sauce unless you ask directly.

None of this means Japan is unsafe for coeliac travellers — far from it. Rice is the staple grain, and there’s more naturally wheat-free food here than in many Western countries. But it does mean your card needs to work harder than “I’m on a gluten-free diet” ever could.

What makes a card actually useful

A card that gets results usually does three things: states that this is a medical condition, not a preference; explains that even a trace amount causes harm; and — critically — gives staff a specific, checkable action rather than a vague category.

Compare two approaches:

Weak: “I am gluten-free.” (Often read as a preference. Staff may remove obvious bread but miss soy sauce, miso, or shared frying oil.)

Stronger: A card that states the medical condition, specifies that trace amounts cause illness, and asks a direct question about wheat and soy sauce — because soy sauce is the single most common hidden source of wheat in Japanese cooking.

Here is wording built from phrases a native speaker has checked, which you can write on a physical card or show on your phone:

セリアック病のため、少量の小麦でも体調が悪くなります。 Seriakku-byō no tame, shōryō no komugi demo taichō ga waruku narimasu. “I have coeliac disease, so even a small amount of wheat makes me ill.”

That single sentence does the heavy lifting: it names the condition (not just a diet), and it states that small amounts are dangerous — which directly counters the “a little is probably fine” assumption.

Follow it with a direct question about soy sauce, since this is where most wheat exposure actually happens:

この料理に小麦や醤油は入っていますか？ Kono ryōri ni komugi ya shōyu wa haitte imasu ka? “Does this dish contain wheat or soy sauce?”

If the answer is yes and you want to understand why, you can add:

醤油にも小麦が入っているので、醤油も食べられません。 Shōyu ni mo komugi ga haitte iru node, shōyu mo taberaremasen. “Soy sauce also contains wheat, so I can’t have soy sauce either.”

This line is useful because many staff genuinely don’t know soy sauce contains wheat — it’s such a basic pantry item that its ingredients aren’t something people think about day to day.

Asking about frying oil and shared equipment

Tempura, tonkatsu, karaage, and many set-meal side dishes are fried, and fryers are very often shared between breaded items (which use wheat-based panko) and other food. This is one of the highest-risk cross-contact points in a Japanese kitchen, so it deserves its own question:

揚げ油はほかの料理と共用ですか？パン粉や衣のついたものと同じ油で揚げていますか？ Age-abura wa hoka no ryōri to kyōyō desu ka? Panko ya koromo no tsuita mono to onaji abura de agete imasu ka? “Is the frying oil shared with other dishes? Is it fried in the same oil as breaded or battered food?”

If staff say yes, it’s reasonable to decline that dish rather than ask them to improvise a separate batch — small kitchens often don’t have the capacity to guarantee a truly separate fryer, even with good intentions.

Opening the conversation and closing it politely

A card works best as part of a short spoken exchange, not a silent handover. Starting with a general opener signals that this is a real conversation, not just a piece of paper:

すみません、食事制限があります。 Sumimasen, shokuji seigen ga arimasu. “Excuse me, I have dietary restrictions.”

Then show your coeliac-specific line, ask about wheat and soy sauce, and if needed, ask about the frying oil. If staff confirm something is safe to your satisfaction, a warm close goes a long way in a culture where service staff appreciate clear, polite communication:

大丈夫です、ありがとうございます。 Daijōbu desu, arigatō gozaimasu. “That’s fine, thank you.”

If you’re unsure whether a dish is genuinely wheat-free, it’s always fine to ask more directly what’s in it:

この料理に何が入っているか教えてください。 Kono ryōri ni nani ga haitte iru ka oshiete kudasai. “Please tell me what is in this dish.”

Carrying your own wheat-free soy sauce

Because standard soy sauce is so deeply embedded in Japanese cooking, many coeliac travellers carry a small bottle of wheat-free (often tamari-style) soy sauce and offer it to the kitchen rather than hoping the restaurant stocks one. Two phrases help here:

小麦不使用の醤油はありますか？ Komugi fushiyō no shōyu wa arimasu ka? “Do you have wheat-free soy sauce?”

持参した醤油を使ってもいいですか？ Jisan shita shōyu o tsukatte mo ii desu ka? “May I use the soy sauce I brought?”

This single habit solves one of the biggest recurring risks of eating out in Japan. For more on what else is worth packing alongside your card, see our gluten-free Japan packing list.

A card is a starting point, not a guarantee

However well-worded your card is, no dish, restaurant or kitchen can be called “safe” or “guaranteed” gluten-free — only you and the staff in front of you, on that day, can assess the actual risk based on what they tell you. Shared equipment, last-minute substitutions, and simple miscommunication can all undermine even a careful kitchen’s best intentions. Always confirm in person, every single time, even at a place you’ve visited before, since staff, suppliers and recipes can change.

Quick checklist

  • Lead with the coeliac-specific line, not a generic “gluten-free” statement — it signals a medical condition, not a diet choice.
  • Always ask specifically about soy sauce; regular shoyu contains wheat and is in far more dishes than you’d expect.
  • Ask about shared frying oil before ordering anything fried or battered.
  • Carry wheat-free soy sauce and offer it to the kitchen rather than assuming they stock one.
  • Treat every card interaction as a conversation — open politely, confirm understanding, and don’t rely on a nod alone.
  • Re-confirm every time, even at restaurants you’ve visited before; staff, suppliers and recipes change.
