Is Miso Gluten-Free? Rice, Barley and Soybean Miso Explained

October 31, 2026 · 7 min read

Why “is miso gluten-free” doesn’t have one answer

Miso is not a single product — it’s a category, like “cheese.” Some miso is made purely from soybeans. Some is made with rice. A lot of it, especially in southern Japan, is made with barley. And barley contains gluten, just like wheat does.

That matters because Japan’s mandatory allergen labelling system does not require barley to be declared. The nine allergens that must appear on packaged food are egg, milk, wheat, buckwheat, peanut, shrimp, crab, walnut and (from 2026) cashew — we cover the full list in our guide to Japan’s mandatory allergen labels. Barley isn’t on it. So a miso tub can be completely barley-based and still carry no allergen warning you’d recognise as gluten-related. The wheat line on the label tells you nothing about barley.

The good news: miso’s three main types are usually distinguishable by name, even if you can’t read much Japanese. Learning three words will do more for your safety than trying to parse a full ingredient panel.

The three types of miso, and why they differ

All miso starts the same way: soybeans are fermented with koji, a mould culture that’s grown on a starch base. That starch base is what determines whether gluten is in the picture.

Rice miso (kome miso, 米味噌) — koji grown on rice. This is the most common type nationwide, including most everyday white (shiro) and red (aka) miso sold in supermarkets. Rice itself contains no gluten, so a straightforward rice miso with no other additions is typically gluten-free. The caveat is “typically” — some blended products mix rice koji with a little barley koji, or add seasonings that bring wheat back in (more on that below).

Barley miso (mugi miso, 麦味噌) — koji grown on barley. This is traditional in Kyushu, Shikoku and parts of western Japan, and it’s widely sold and loved for its sweeter, earthier flavour. It is not suitable for a gluten-free or coeliac diet, because barley is a gluten-containing grain. Crucially, because barley isn’t a mandatory allergen in Japan, the packaging may have no gluten-related warning at all — the only clue is the product name itself.

Soybean miso (mame miso, 豆味噌) — koji grown on soybeans, with no rice or barley involved. This style is associated with the Tokai region (Aichi, Mie, Gifu) and tends to be dark, dense and quite strong in flavour. Because there’s no grain-based koji at all, a plain soybean miso is often the least risky of the three from a gluten standpoint — but “plain” is the key word, since some products still add alcohol, dashi powder or flavour pastes that can reintroduce wheat.

Kome miso (rice) Rice Usually none, but check additives Nationwide, most supermarket miso

Reading the label: what to actually look for

Because barley isn’t a mandatory allergen, you can’t rely on a “contains” statement to rule it out. Instead:

  1. Check the product name first. Japanese miso packaging almost always states the type somewhere on the front — look for 米味噌 (rice), 麦味噌 (barley) or 豆味噌 (soybean). If the front label just says 味噌 with no qualifier, the ingredient list on the back is your next stop.
  2. Scan the ingredient list for 麦 or 大麦. These refer to barley. They may appear even on a miso that doesn’t call itself “mugi miso” if barley koji was blended in alongside rice koji — some everyday supermarket miso does exactly this for flavour balance.
  3. Look for wheat (小麦) too. Some miso-based seasoning pastes, instant miso soup bases, and “miso dare” (miso sauce blends used for dishes like dengaku or miso-marinated grilled food) add soy sauce, wheat-based starch, or alcohol brewed from a wheat/barley mix. Wheat must be labelled, so this one is at least mandatory — but it’s easy to miss if you’re only scanning for the word “miso.”
  4. Watch for “jōzō alcohol” (醸造アルコール) in the ingredients. It’s often made from sugarcane or corn, but can occasionally be grain-based. Manufacturers aren’t required to specify the source unless it’s one of the nine mandatory allergens, so if you have coeliac disease and want certainty, contacting the manufacturer through their official customer service line is the only way to confirm.
  5. Pure soybean miso ingredient lists are usually short — soybeans, salt, koji, sometimes alcohol. A long ingredient list with multiple seasoning additions is a sign to read more carefully, not a reason to avoid it outright.

If you’re buying packaged miso at a supermarket or depachika food hall, this label-reading approach is the same skill you’d use for any processed food — we go into more depth on scanning Japanese packaging in our guide to reading konbini and supermarket labels.

Miso soup at restaurants: the labelling gap

Restaurant food in Japan — including the miso soup that comes free with set meals — has no mandatory allergen labelling at all. The miso paste used in a restaurant kitchen is whatever the kitchen buys in bulk, and staff may not know whether it’s rice, barley or soybean-based unless they check the tub themselves. Many restaurants use a blended, mass-produced miso that isn’t barley-free, and some use their own house blend mixing multiple types for flavour.

This is one of the clearest cases where you have to ask rather than guess. A useful opening is:

すみません、食事制限があります。 Sumimasen, shokuji seigen ga arimasu. “Excuse me, I have dietary restrictions.”

Followed by asking what’s in the dish directly:

この料理に何が入っているか教えてください。 Kono ryōri ni nani ga haitte iru ka oshiete kudasai. “Please tell me what is in this dish.”

If you want to narrow in on wheat specifically — which covers the mandatory-labelled risk, even though it won’t catch barley by name — you can ask:

小麦は使っていますか？ Komugi wa tsukatte imasu ka? “Does this contain wheat?”

There currently isn’t a standardised Japanese phrase for asking specifically about barley that we’re confident enough in to publish here, so for now the safest approach with a serious coeliac diagnosis is to treat any restaurant miso soup as unconfirmed unless staff can tell you the miso is rice- or soybean-based, or unless the menu explicitly states a gluten-free option. For coeliac disease, it’s worth explaining the severity clearly:

セリアック病のため、少量の小麦でも体調が悪くなります。 Seriakku-byō no tame, shōryō no komugi demo taichō ga waruku narimasu. “I have coeliac disease, so even a small amount of wheat makes me ill.”

Staff are far more likely to double-check the miso tub and ask their own kitchen colleagues when they understand this isn’t a preference. If the answer is uncertain, it’s completely reasonable to decline the soup — most set meals can be eaten without it, and saying so is simple:

大丈夫です、ありがとうございます。 Daijōbu desu, arigatō gozaimasu. “That’s fine, thank you.”

Cross-contact and shared equipment

Even a soybean-only miso made in a facility that also produces barley miso can pick up trace gluten through shared tanks, scoops or packing lines, in the same way flour dust travels around a bakery. Large miso manufacturers often make several product lines in one factory. If your reaction to gluten is severe, this is worth factoring in alongside the ingredient list — a company’s customer service line can usually tell you whether a specific product is made on a dedicated line, though many small-scale producers won’t have this level of documentation.

This is the same logic that applies to shared frying oil, shared grills, and shared sauce pots elsewhere in Japanese kitchens — it’s covered in more detail in our guide to soy sauce and wheat in Japan, since miso and shoyu are made by closely related fermentation processes and face many of the same hidden-wheat issues.

A quick word on miso-based dishes beyond soup

Miso turns up well beyond the soup bowl — in glazes (miso dengaku), marinades for grilled fish, dressing for salads, and flavouring in simmered dishes. Each of these carries the same type-dependent risk, plus the added question of what else was mixed into the sauce (mirin, sake, soy sauce, or a dash of flour to thicken it). Treat miso-based sauces at restaurants the same way you’d treat any made-to-order sauce: ask rather than assume, and don’t let a dish’s reputation as “clean” or “simple” stand in for an actual answer from the kitchen. If you’re travelling through Kyoto, this comes up often with kaiseki and temple-style set meals, which we look at more closely in our guide to gluten-free eating in Kyoto.

If severe gluten exposure is a medical concern for you, always carry any prescribed medication and follow your doctor’s guidance in addition to the precautions in this article.

Quick checklist

  • Miso type matters more than the word “miso” itself — rice and soybean miso are usually gluten-free; barley miso (mugi miso) is not.
  • Barley isn’t a mandatory allergen in Japan, so packaging may not flag it — check the product name (米味噌 / 麦味噌 / 豆味噌) and ingredient list for 麦 or 大麦.
  • Wheat (小麦) is mandatory on packaged food, but miso-based sauces, dashi bases and instant soup packets can still hide it in added seasonings.
  • Restaurant miso soup has no mandatory labelling — ask staff directly and explain that even a small amount matters if you have coeliac disease.
  • Shared factory equipment can bring trace gluten into an otherwise barley-free miso; contact manufacturers directly if you need certainty.
  • When in doubt, it’s fine to skip the soup — say so politely and move on with the rest of your meal.
