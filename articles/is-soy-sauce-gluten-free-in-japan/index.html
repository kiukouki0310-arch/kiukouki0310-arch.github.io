Is Soy Sauce Gluten-Free in Japan? What Coeliacs Need to Know

October 12, 2026 · 7 min read

No. Standard Japanese soy sauce is not gluten-free. The most common type, koikuchi shōyu (dark soy sauce), is brewed from soybeans and wheat in roughly equal amounts, and it is the default bottle on almost every table and in almost every kitchen in Japan.

That matters far more in Japan than at home, because soy sauce is not just a condiment. It is a base ingredient in sauces, broths, marinades and snacks. If you are coeliac or need to avoid gluten, understanding where soy sauce hides is the single most useful thing you can learn before your trip.

Why Japanese soy sauce contains wheat

Japanese soy sauce is made by fermenting soybeans with roasted, crushed wheat. The wheat adds sweetness and aroma, and it is a large part of why Japanese soy sauce tastes the way it does. Fermentation does not reliably remove gluten, so regular soy sauce should be treated as a gluten source.

The main styles you will meet:

Type Japanese Wheat? Where you will see it
Dark soy sauce 濃口醤油 (koikuchi) Yes, a lot Table bottles, most cooking
Light-coloured soy sauce 薄口醤油 (usukuchi) Yes Kansai cooking, clear soups, simmered dishes
Tamari たまり醤油 (tamari) Often little or none, but many brands add wheat Sushi, sashimi, some regional cooking
White soy sauce 白醤油 (shiro) Yes, mostly wheat Light-coloured dishes, some high-end cooking
Wheat-free soy sauce 小麦不使用 (komugi fushiyō) No wheat used Some supermarkets and specialist shops

Tamari is not automatically safe. Traditional tamari uses mostly soybeans, but many commercial tamari products in Japan still contain wheat. Only trust a product that is clearly labelled wheat-free or gluten-free, and check every time.

Where soy sauce hides in Japanese food

This is where most gluten exposure happens: not from the bottle on the table, but from soy sauce already cooked into a dish.

  • Tare (seasoning sauces): yakitori glaze, teriyaki, eel sauce (kabayaki no tare), and the seasoning in many ramen bowls
  • Tsuyu (noodle broth and dipping sauce): used with soba, udon, tempura and many hot pots
  • Ponzu: citrus dipping sauce that usually contains soy sauce
  • Simmered dishes (nimono): vegetables, fish and tofu simmered in dashi, soy sauce, mirin and sugar
  • Sukiyaki and many nabe (hot pots): the cooking broth is soy-sauce-based
  • Marinades: karaage (fried chicken) is often marinated in soy sauce before the floured coating
  • Rice crackers (senbei): many are brushed with soy sauce
  • Seasoned rice and toppings: furikake, seasoned seaweed and some onigiri fillings
  • “Clear” soups: even a light-looking soup can contain usukuchi soy sauce

A useful rule of thumb: if a Japanese dish is brown, glossy, savoury-sweet or dipped in something, assume soy sauce until you are told otherwise.

Reading labels in shops

Japan has mandatory allergen labelling for packaged foods, and wheat (小麦, komugi) is one of the ingredients that must be declared. This makes supermarket and convenience-store shopping much more manageable than eating out.

What to look for on a package:

  • 小麦 (komugi) — wheat. If you see this anywhere in the ingredients or allergen box, avoid it.
  • A bracketed note after soy sauce in the ingredients list stating it contains wheat. Manufacturers phrase this a few different ways, so read the full ingredient line rather than scanning for one fixed phrase.
  • 小麦不使用 (komugi fushiyō) — “no wheat used”.
  • グルテンフリー (guruten furī) — “gluten-free”. Standards for this claim are not the same as in every country, so read the full ingredient list too.

Two important gaps:

  1. Barley is not a mandatory allergen in Japan. Look out for 大麦 (ōmugi, barley) and 麦芽 (bakuga, malt). Barley tea (mugicha, 麦茶) is served free in many restaurants and is not gluten-free.
  2. Restaurant food has no mandatory allergen labelling. Some chains publish allergen charts, but small, independent restaurants usually do not. You will need to ask.

Eating out: how to ask

Many Japanese staff understand “wheat” (小麦) well, because wheat allergy is common in Japan. What they may not realise is that soy sauce, miso and many sauces contain wheat. Coeliac disease is also much less familiar in Japan than wheat allergy, so it helps to describe what you need rather than only naming the condition.

Useful phrases:

  • 小麦が食べられません。 Komugi ga taberaremasen. “I can’t eat wheat.”
  • 醤油にも小麦が入っているので、醤油も食べられません。 Shōyu ni mo komugi ga haitte iru node, shōyu mo taberaremasen. “Soy sauce also contains wheat, so I can’t have soy sauce either.”
  • この料理に醤油は入っていますか？ Kono ryōri ni shōyu wa haitte imasu ka? “Does this dish contain soy sauce?”
  • 少量でも体調が悪くなります。 Shōryō demo taichō ga waruku narimasu. “Even a small amount makes me ill.”
  • 持参した醤油を使ってもいいですか？ Jisan shita shōyu o tsukatte mo ii desu ka? “May I use the soy sauce I brought?”

Showing these phrases in writing usually works better than saying them aloud. Written Japanese removes pronunciation problems, gives staff time to read carefully, and lets them show it to the kitchen.

Bring your own gluten-free soy sauce

Many coeliac travellers carry a small bottle of wheat-free soy sauce or certified gluten-free tamari. It solves the most common problem, the table condiment, especially at sushi and sashimi restaurants.

Practical tips:

  • Buy it in Japan. Larger supermarkets and natural-food shops often stock products labelled 小麦不使用 or グルテンフリー.
  • Ask before using it. Using your own condiment is usually accepted if you ask politely (see the phrase above), but it is still a request, not a right.
  • It does not fix cooked food. Your own soy sauce helps with sushi and sashimi, but it does nothing for sauces, broths or marinades already in the dish.

Safer types of meals to look for

No category of food is automatically safe, and cross-contact is always possible. Still, some meals are easier to adapt than others:

  • Sashimi and sushi with your own wheat-free soy sauce. Ask the chef to skip brushed-on sauces (nikiri) and avoid imitation crab (kanikama) and sweet egg (tamago), which may contain wheat.
  • Plain grilled dishes seasoned with salt. Yakitori shio (salt) style instead of tare. Ask whether the grill is shared with sauced items.
  • Plain rice and simple onigiri. Choose fillings like salted salmon or umeboshi, and check the label for 小麦.
  • Dedicated gluten-free restaurants and bakeries. A small but growing number exist in large cities. Search in English and Japanese (グルテンフリー) and check recent reviews.

Quick checklist

  • Treat all regular Japanese soy sauce as containing wheat, including tamari unless it is labelled wheat-free.
  • Remember that soy sauce hides in tare, tsuyu, ponzu, marinades, simmered dishes and rice crackers.
  • On packaged food, look for 小麦 (wheat), 大麦 (barley) and 麦芽 (malt).
  • Avoid barley tea (麦茶), which is often served free.
  • Show written Japanese to staff rather than relying on spoken requests.
  • Carry wheat-free soy sauce for sushi and sashimi, and ask before using it.
  • If your reaction is severe, follow your doctor’s advice and carry your medication. Recipes change, so confirm with staff every time.
