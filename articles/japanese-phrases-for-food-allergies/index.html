Japanese Phrases for Food Allergies: What to Say and When

October 29, 2026 · 6 min read

If you have a food allergy and you’re heading to Japan, the question isn’t really “do people speak English?” — it’s “do I know the right words to make a kitchen take this seriously?” A few well-chosen Japanese phrases, said clearly and in the right order, do far more for your safety than a long nervous explanation in English to a busy server who may not follow all of it.

This guide gives you a small, deliberate set of phrases — the ones that actually change how a kitchen handles your order — and explains when to use each one and why the wording matters.

Why the exact wording matters in Japan

Japanese service culture leans heavily toward reassurance. Staff are trained to make guests comfortable, and that sometimes means a quick “daijōbu” (it’s fine) before anyone has actually checked with the kitchen. That isn’t dishonesty — it’s politeness colliding with a genuinely complicated food system. Dashi defaults to bonito, sauces are pre-mixed in the morning, and a single fryer often cooks shrimp, chicken, and vegetables side by side.

Vague English like “I’m allergic to shellfish, is this okay?” invites a vague, reassuring answer. A short, specific Japanese phrase that names the kitchen process — not just the dish — invites a real answer, because it tells staff exactly what to go and check.

Start every conversation the same way

Before you name your allergen, flag that this is a dietary restriction conversation, not small talk about preferences:

すみません、食事制限があります。 Sumimasen, shokuji seigen ga arimasu. “Excuse me, I have dietary restrictions.”

This sentence does one job: it tells the person in front of you to switch into “allergy mode” and, often, to go get a manager or the chart described below. Say it calmly, make eye contact, and wait for a response before continuing — don’t rush straight into details.

Name your specific allergen

Japan’s packaged-food labelling law currently requires nine allergens to be marked: egg, milk, wheat, buckwheat, peanut, shrimp, crab, walnut, and — from April 2026, with a transition period running until March 2028 — cashew nut. During that transition, older stock may legally still be sold without cashew listed, so don’t assume an unlabelled product is cashew-free. Barley isn’t mandatory at all, and macadamia and pistachio are only “recommended,” meaning many products won’t mention them even if present.

That labelling list is a useful guide for which allergens have an established single Japanese word you can drop into this phrase:

〇〇のアレルギーがあります。 ___ no arerugī ga arimasu. “I’m allergic to ___.”

Allergen Japanese word Romaji
Egg 卵 tamago
Milk 乳 nyū
Wheat 小麦 komugi
Buckwheat そば soba
Peanut 落花生 rakkasei
Walnut くるみ kurumi
Shrimp えび ebi
Crab かに kani

So a shrimp allergy sounds like: Ebi no arerugī ga arimasu. (“I’m allergic to shrimp.”)

If your allergen is cashew nut, sesame, or anything else not on this list, don’t improvise a sentence — point to the written word on your allergy card or phone instead, and describe the allergen in English to staff who understand it, or show a card from a reliable translation source. Explain how serious it is — don’t let them downplay it

This is the phrase that stops a well-meaning “it’s probably fine” in its tracks:

少量でも体調が悪くなります。 Shōryō demo taichō ga waruku narimasu. “Even a small amount makes me ill.”

Follow it, if your allergy is severe, with:

重いアレルギーなので、調理器具の共用も心配です。 Omoi arerugī na node, chōri kigu no kyōyō mo shinpai desu. “My allergy is severe, so shared cooking equipment is also a concern.”

This second phrase is the one that gets to the real risk in Japanese kitchens: shared tongs, shared fryer oil, a wok that just cooked shrimp before your vegetables go in. Many dishes can be made allergen-free in the ingredient list but still carry a real cross-contact risk from the equipment. Naming that directly, rather than assuming it’s covered, is often the difference between a dish that’s genuinely workable and one that just looks fine on paper.

Ask what’s actually in the dish

Don’t rely on the menu description alone — ask directly:

この料理に何が入っているか教えてください。 Kono ryōri ni nani ga haitte iru ka oshiete kudasai. “Please tell me what is in this dish.”

This is deliberately open-ended, which is useful because it prompts staff to think through the whole recipe rather than just answering a yes/no question about one ingredient you happened to name. Follow up with a request to remove something if needed:

抜いてもらうことはできますか？ Nuite morau koto wa dekimasu ka? “Could you leave it out?”

Ask for the allergen chart

Many chain restaurants, department store restaurants, and larger family-dining places keep a printed allergen chart (arerugī-hyō) listing the nine mandatory allergens per menu item, even though this isn’t legally required for restaurant food the way it is for packaged products. It’s always worth asking:

アレルギー表はありますか？ Arerugī-hyō wa arimasu ka? “Do you have an allergen chart?”

If one exists, it will usually be a laminated sheet with a grid of dishes against the nine allergens — read it yourself rather than relying solely on a verbal summary, since staff reading it aloud can occasionally skip a column. Close the conversation clearly

Once you’re satisfied with the answer, confirm it:

大丈夫です、ありがとうございます。 Daijōbu desu, arigatō gozaimasu. “That’s fine, thank you.”

This matters more than it seems. Saying this clearly signals that the conversation is finished and the order can proceed — without it, staff may keep checking or hesitate to bring your food, unsure if you’re still deciding.

A typical order of phrases, start to finish

  1. Flag the topic: Sumimasen, shokuji seigen ga arimasu.
  2. Name the allergen: [Allergen] no arerugī ga arimasu.
  3. State the stakes: Shōryō demo taichō ga waruku narimasu.
  4. If severe, add the equipment concern: Omoi arerugī na node, chōri kigu no kyōyō mo shinpai desu.
  5. Ask what’s in the dish you’re considering: Kono ryōri ni nani ga haitte iru ka oshiete kudasai.
  6. If needed, ask for an allergen chart: Arerugī-hyō wa arimasu ka?
  7. Request a removal if possible: Nuite morau koto wa dekimasu ka?
  8. Confirm: Daijōbu desu, arigatō gozaimasu.

You won’t need all eight every time — a quiet café visit might only need steps 1, 2, and 8 — but having the full sequence ready means you’re never stuck mid-conversation.

A note on severity

None of this replaces medical preparation. If your allergy is severe, always carry your prescribed medication (such as an epinephrine auto-injector) with you at every meal, and follow your doctor’s guidance for your specific condition — these phrases are communication tools, not a substitute for that.

Quick checklist

  • Open with Sumimasen, shokuji seigen ga arimasu before naming anything specific.
  • Use the allergen table above to state your exact allergen in Japanese.
  • Say Shōryō demo taichō ga waruku narimasu to make clear this isn’t a preference.
  • For severe allergies, add the shared-equipment phrase — fryers, grills, and tongs are common cross-contact points.
  • Ask Kono ryōri ni nani ga haitte iru ka oshiete kudasai rather than trusting the menu description alone.
  • Ask for an arerugī-hyō (allergen chart) where available, and read it yourself.
  • Confirm with Daijōbu desu, arigatō gozaimasu once you’re satisfied — this signals the order can proceed.
  • Carry a written card or phone translation for allergens not covered by these phrases (cashew, sesame, and others outside Japan’s nine mandatory labels).
  • Always carry your prescribed medication and follow your doctor’s guidance if your allergy is severe.
