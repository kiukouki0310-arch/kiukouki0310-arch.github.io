Coeliac at a Ryokan: How to Request Gluten-Free Kaiseki

November 6, 2026 · 6 min read

If you’re coeliac and booking a ryokan stay, the question isn’t just “can they do gluten-free” — it’s “when do I need to ask, and how specifically.” The honest answer is: before you book, or at the very latest when you book. Kaiseki, the multi-course dinner that’s the whole point of a ryokan stay, is planned and often partly prepared days in advance. Walking in and asking the kitchen to improvise on the night rarely works the way it would at an a-la-carte restaurant.

This matters more at a ryokan than almost anywhere else in Japan, because dinner (and often breakfast) is included, served in your room or a private dining area, and built around a set seasonal menu that the chef has already shopped and prepped for. There’s usually no menu to scan and no substitute dish waiting in the kitchen — what arrives is what was planned for your table.

Why kaiseki can’t be adjusted on the spot

A restaurant kitchen cooks to order from a stocked pantry, so a server can often ask the chef to leave something out or swap an ingredient with a few minutes’ notice. A ryokan kaiseki dinner is different: ingredients are often ordered for specific guests days ahead, some components are prepared the morning of (or even the day before), and the courses are plated to a fixed presentation. Many small ryokan kitchens are also staffed by one or two people cooking for every guest in the building that night — there isn’t spare capacity to rebuild a course from scratch at 6pm.

This is why the single most useful thing you can do is contact the ryokan as early as possible, ideally when making the reservation, and again a few days before you arrive.

Where wheat typically hides in a kaiseki dinner

Coeliac disease means even trace amounts of wheat matter, and kaiseki is full of small sauces and seasonings where wheat is easy to miss. Typical risk points include:

  • Soy sauce (koikuchi shōyu) — used throughout kaiseki, from dipping sauces to glazes to the seasoning base of simmered dishes. Regular Japanese soy sauce is brewed with wheat; our guide to soy sauce and gluten explains the brewing process and why tamari isn’t automatically safe either.
  • Dashi and tsuyu — some bonito or kelp dashi is seasoned or stretched with soy sauce, and many ready-made tsuyu bases contain wheat.
  • Miso — used in soups, glazes and dressings; some miso is made with barley or wheat koji. See our miso and gluten breakdown for how it varies by type and brand.
  • Simmered dishes (nimono) — the cooking liquid is almost always soy-sauce based.
  • Tempura and other fried courses — the batter itself may use wheat flour, and the frying oil is frequently shared with other breaded or battered items, risking cross-contact even if a flour-free version is made.
  • Thickened sauces — some use wheat flour as a thickener rather than potato or corn starch (katakuriko), so appearance alone won’t tell you.
  • Vinegared and pickled side dishes — some use seasoned vinegar or dressings containing soy sauce or wheat-derived ingredients.

Dessert is sometimes the safer-looking course, but seasonal wagashi can still contain wheat flour depending on the type. Our guide to gluten-free Japanese snacks is useful background if you want to recognise which traditional sweets are more likely to be rice-based.

None of this means kaiseki is off-limits — many ryokan can and do adjust the menu for coeliac guests — but it does mean every component needs checking, and that checking needs to start well before the kitchen starts prepping.

When to contact the ryokan

Timing What to do
When booking Mention coeliac disease in the reservation notes or booking email, in English and, if possible, with a short Japanese explanation.
1–2 weeks before Follow up by phone or email if you haven’t had confirmation of how the kitchen plans to adjust the meal.
2–3 days before Send a reminder, especially for ryokan without English-speaking staff on every shift.
On check-in Repeat the request in person with front-desk or nakai (room attendant) staff, who will relay it to the kitchen.
At each meal Reconfirm briefly before the first course arrives, since kitchen staff can change shift to shift.

Many ryokan booking sites and travel agents allow a free-text request field — use it, but don’t rely on it alone. A direct phone call or email in advance, followed by a polite reminder close to your stay, is far more reliable than a single note typed into a booking form.

What to say

Carrying a written note is genuinely useful here, because kaiseki involves kitchen staff who may never interact with guests directly. Our printable dietary card guide walks through building one; for a ryokan stay, lead with the clearest possible statement of why this matters:

セリアック病のため、少量の小麦でも体調が悪くなります。 Seriakku-byō no tame, shōryō no komugi demo taichō ga waruku narimasu. “I have coeliac disease, so even a small amount of wheat makes me ill.”

This is more precise than simply saying you “can’t eat wheat,” and it signals that this isn’t a preference. Follow with the soy sauce connection, since staff may not immediately link shōyu to komugi:

醤油にも小麦が入っているので、醤油も食べられません。 Shōyu ni mo komugi ga haitte iru node, shōyu mo taberaremasen. “Soy sauce also contains wheat, so I can’t have soy sauce either.”

If you want to ask about a wheat-free alternative for dipping sauces or glazes:

小麦不使用の醤油はありますか？ Komugi fushiyō no shōyu wa arimasu ka? “Do you have wheat-free soy sauce?”

And because frying oil is a genuine cross-contact risk if the kitchen also serves breaded or battered dishes to other guests that night:

揚げ油はほかの料理と共用ですか？パン粉や衣のついたものと同じ油で揚げていますか？ Age-abura wa hoka no ryōri to kyōyō desu ka? Panko ya koromo no tsuita mono to onaji abura de agete imasu ka? “Is the frying oil shared with other dishes? Is it fried in the same oil as breaded or battered food?”

If a dish simply needs one ingredient removed rather than a full substitution:

抜いてもらうことはできますか？ Nuite morau koto wa dekimasu ka? “Could you leave it out?”

Breakfast needs the same attention

Traditional ryokan breakfasts look simpler than the kaiseki dinner — grilled fish, rice, miso soup, pickles, a small simmered dish — but miso soup and pickles both carry the same wheat and soy sauce risks as dinner. Don’t assume breakfast is automatically lower-risk; ask about it using the same phrases, ideally in the same advance message as your dinner request.

If you’re staying in a kaiseki-heavy region

Kyoto in particular has a dense concentration of ryokan built around refined kaiseki dinners, and the dynamics above apply there especially strongly. If your trip includes Kyoto, our gluten-free Kyoto guide covers temple food and kaiseki together and is worth reading alongside this one.

What a good response from the ryokan looks like

A ryokan that’s used to dietary requests will typically confirm in writing which specific courses they can adjust, ask you follow-up questions about severity, and sometimes offer a modified multi-course menu rather than simply removing items from the standard one. A vague “we’ll do our best” with no detail is a signal to follow up again before you travel, or to call and ask directly which dashi and seasonings the kitchen uses. Given that this is coeliac disease and not a mild intolerance, it’s reasonable to say so plainly:

少量でも体調が悪くなります。 Shōryō demo taichō ga waruku narimasu. “Even a small amount makes me ill.”

If you carry emergency medication for your condition, bring it with you and follow your doctor’s guidance for managing accidental exposure while travelling — this is general travel information, not medical advice.

Quick checklist

  • Mention coeliac disease when booking, not just on arrival
  • Follow up by phone or email 1–2 weeks before your stay, and again a few days out
  • Ask specifically about soy sauce, dashi, miso and frying oil — not just “wheat”
  • Request details on which courses can be adjusted, in writing if possible
  • Reconfirm with staff at check-in and briefly before each meal
  • Carry a written Japanese explanation card as backup
  • Check breakfast as carefully as dinner — miso soup and pickles carry the same risks
  • Carry your medication and follow your doctor’s guidance for your condition while travelling
